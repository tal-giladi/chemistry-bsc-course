How to use this, and why

Procedure
  1. Precipitation: weigh the sample, dissolve it in about 150 mL of water with a little HNO₃, heat, add AgNO₃ slowly with stirring until a little excess, digest hot for an hour, cool in the dark.
  2. Filter through a sintered-glass crucible already dried to constant mass; wash with several small portions of dilute HNO₃; dry at 130 °C, cool in a desiccator, weigh; repeat until two weighings agree within 0.2–0.3 mg.
  3. Electrogravimetry: weigh the clean, dry Pt gauze cathode; electrolyse the stirred, acidified solution at a controlled cathode potential; test for completeness; lift the cathode out with the current still on, rinse with water and ethanol, dry briefly, weigh.
  4. Coulometric titration: generate the titrant at constant current, watch the end point, stop; the charge Q = I t replaces the burette reading.
  5. Volatilisation: heat, cool the same way every time, weigh, repeat to constant mass.
Why each step is done
  • Low relative supersaturation (slow, hot, dilute) gives few large crystals that filter well and trap fewer impurities; digestion lets small crystals dissolve and large ones grow.
  • A small excess of Ag⁺ lowers the solubility of AgCl (common-ion effect); a large excess is adsorbed on the solid and co-precipitates.
  • Pure water peptises AgCl back into a colloid that runs through the filter; a dilute volatile electrolyte (HNO₃) keeps it coagulated and leaves nothing on drying.
  • Constant mass, not a fixed time, proves the water is gone. A warm crucible reads light (rising air) and a dry solid picks up water while it cools in room air; a desiccator prevents that.
  • At a cathode held below +0.10 V vs SHE, copper is deposited to better than 99.99 %; much more negative potentials also reduce H⁺ (wasting charge, giving a spongy deposit).
  • Faraday's law, n = Q/(zF), counts moles only at 100 % current efficiency; a blank removes charge used by impurities in the reagents.
Safety reasoning
  • Silver nitrate stains skin and is toxic to aquatic life: gloves, glasses, collect silver residues for recovery.
  • Hot nitric and sulfuric acids burn: glasses, gloves, fume hood for heating.
  • Electrolysis cells make hydrogen at the cathode and oxygen at the anode: no flames, good ventilation, and never touch the electrodes while the supply is on.
  • A hot oven and tray burn: oven mitts and a heat-proof board. Sodium carbonate irritates eyes (GHS H319): glasses.
What can go wrong
  • Fast, cold precipitation: fine particles pass the filter (low) and trap impurities (high).
  • Washing with pure water: peptisation losses. No digestion: more of everything.
  • Stopping after one drying, or weighing warm or after cooling in moist air.
  • Too positive a cathode potential: copper left in solution. Too negative: hydrogen evolution, low current efficiency, flaking deposit. Switching off before lifting the cathode: copper redissolves in the nitric acid.
  • Coulometry at high current: some charge oxidises water instead of iodide; overshooting the end point because the clock runs fast.
