How to use this, and why

The rule
  1. Each level i holds gi states; a molecule in a bath at T occupies it with probability pi = gie−Ei/kBT/q.
  2. Ratios need no q: N₁/N₀ = (g₁/g₀)e−ε/kBT, with ε/kB = 1.438777 cm K × ε in cm⁻¹.
  3. The mean energy, heat capacity and entropy follow from q; the two-level CV has a peak (the Schottky anomaly).
  4. Measured the other way round: ln[(N₁/g₁)/(N₀/g₀)] against 1/T is a straight line of slope −ε/kB.
Why each step is done
  • Degeneracy can beat the exponential, so always count states, not levels.
  • Heating alone can only bring the upper level towards (g₁/g₀) times the lower one; more than that (a laser's population inversion) needs pumping.
  • Counting a finite number of molecules gives binomial scatter: ln(ratio) has variance about 1/n₀ + 1/n₁, so cold readings with few excited molecules are the noisiest.
Safety reasoning

Nothing hazardous here. In a lab, populations at high temperature come from flames and furnaces (burns, gas hazards), and halogen atoms from corrosive, toxic Cl₂ and I₂.

What can go wrong
  • Forgetting degeneracy: with g = 1 for both Cl levels you get the wrong limit, the wrong q and the wrong heat capacity.
  • Mixing units: ε/kBT must be dimensionless. With ε in J/mol use R, not kB.
  • Measuring from different zeros for different levels.
  • Giving one molecule a temperature: T belongs to the bath.
Data analysis

Export the counts (T_K, n_lower, n_upper) and fit ln[(n₁/g₁)/(n₀/g₀)] against 1/T. The slope's standard uncertainty from the fit gives u(ε). The fitted gap should agree with the gap you set within its uncertainty; more molecules per reading shrink the scatter as 1/√N.

Populations and heat capacity

How to use this, and why

The rule
  1. Harmonic ladder from v = 0: q = 1/(1 − e−x), x = θvib/T, θvib = hcν̃/kB.
  2. U − U(0) = Rθvib/(ex − 1), CV/R = x²ex/(ex − 1)² (Einstein), S/R = x/(ex − 1) − ln(1 − e−x).
  3. Anharmonic: sum G(v) − G(0) = ωev − ωexev(v + 1) directly, up to the dissociation energy D₀.
Why each step is done
  • q counts the thermally accessible states: it starts at 1 and grows with T.
  • A ladder never saturates, so CV rises to R and stays there; a two-level system fills up and its CV falls back to zero.
  • Soft bonds (I₂) store heat at room temperature, stiff ones (N₂, HCl) do not.
  • Anharmonic levels crowd together and add a little heat capacity at high T: the 0.25 % gap of N₂ at 1000 K.
Safety reasoning

Nothing hazardous here. I₂ vapour is toxic and corrosive; the heat capacities come from spectra, not from heating it.

What can go wrong
  • Using ωe instead of the fundamental ωe − 2ωexe with energies measured from v = 0.
  • Expecting a ladder to show a Schottky peak.
  • Mixing CV and Cp: internal motions add the same amount to both.
Data analysis

Export q, U − U(0), CV/R and S/R on a grid of temperatures and compare with lesson02_partition.py: I₂ gives q = 1.56 and CV,vib = 7.62 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ at 298.15 K; N₂ gives CV,vib/R = 0.4224 at 1000 K.

Populations and the partition function

How to use this, and why

The rule
  1. Exact: qrot = (1/σ) Σ (2J + 1) e−θrotJ(J + 1)/T, θrot = hcB/kB.
  2. High temperature: qrot = T/(σθrot); a better approximation is (T/σθrot)(1 + θrot/3T + θrot²/15T²).
  3. Srot = R(ln q + T d ln q/dT); in the high-temperature limit R(ln q + 1).
  4. For a homonuclear molecule, nuclear-spin statistics weight even and odd J differently (N₂ 6 : 3, H₂ 1 : 3, ¹⁶O₂ only odd J); on average that is the 1/σ.
Why each step is done
  • Use B₀, because nearly every molecule is in v = 0.
  • The symmetry number lowers the rotational entropy by R ln σ (5.76 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ for σ = 2).
  • The high-temperature formula fails when T approaches θrot: about 5 % for HCl at 100 K, and about 10 % for H₂ even at room temperature.
Safety reasoning

Nothing hazardous here. Hydrogen is extremely flammable, CO toxic and HCl corrosive; the spectra behind B are measured in sealed cells.

What can go wrong
  • Forgetting σ: +R ln σ in the entropy of N₂, O₂, I₂.
  • Applying T/σθrot to H₂ or at low temperature.
  • Using Be instead of B₀.
  • Dividing by σ and also applying nuclear-spin weights: that counts the symmetry twice.
Data analysis

Export qrot (exact and high-T) and Srot against T. With lesson03_molecular_q.py: HCl qrot = 20.19 (sum) against 19.85 (high-T) at 298 K; N₂ Srot = 41.18 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Coins per cup

How to use this, and why

The rule
  1. q identical quanta among N oscillators: W(N, q) = (q + N − 1)!/(q!(N − 1)!).
  2. Every microstate of an isolated system is equally likely, so a split is as probable as its share of WAWB.
  3. At the most probable split ∂ln WA/∂qA = ∂ln WB/∂qB: the two solids have the same temperature.
  4. One cup among many is a molecule in a bath: its coins follow a Boltzmann (geometric) distribution.
Why each step is done
  • With 3 + 3 oscillators the even split has only 22 % of the microstates; with 300 + 200 almost all lie near the peak. For a mole no deviation is ever seen.
  • Nothing pushes the coins into the geometric shape; it simply has the most microstates.
Safety reasoning

The kitchen version uses coins: a choking hazard, so keep it away from small children and pets.

What can go wrong
  • Recording before the start is forgotten: the first snapshots still show one coin per cup.
  • Too few snapshots: the tally scatters by several percent; that scatter is the fluctuation that vanishes for 10²³ molecules.
  • Thinking the uneven split is forbidden: it is only rarer.
Data analysis

Export the tally as n,count and run py lesson01_microstates.py my_cups.csv. The ratio N(n + 1)/N(n) = e−ε/kBT gives the temperature of the bath in units of the quantum.
