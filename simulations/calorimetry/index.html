How to use this, and why

Procedure
  1. Weigh the liquid into the dry calorimeter. Put the lid on, insert the thermometer and stirrer.
  2. Read the temperature every 30 s for 3 minutes (the fore-period).
  3. At a noted time, add the second liquid or the solid (or fire the bomb) and keep stirring.
  4. Keep reading every 30 s for at least 5–7 more minutes (the after-period).
  5. Fit a straight line to each period, extrapolate both to the moment of mixing, and take the gap as ΔT.
  6. Calibrate first: hot and cold water give Ccal; benzoic acid gives the bomb's constant. Then run the unknown the same way.
Why each step is done
  • The cup, lid and thermometer absorb heat too; calibration measures them as a heat capacity Ccal.
  • No calorimeter is perfectly insulated. The fore- and after-lines measure the heat leak, and extrapolating to the mixing time corrects for it; the highest or lowest single reading does not.
  • Stirring makes the temperature uniform; a slow thermometer or a slowly dissolving solid makes the rise gradual, which the extrapolation also handles.
  • A bomb works at constant volume, so it measures ΔU; ΔH = ΔU + ΔngasRT.
Safety reasoning
  • Calibration water at about 45 °C is hand-hot, not boiling: scalds start quickly above 50 °C.
  • 1 mol/L HCl and NaOH are corrosive to eyes: safety glasses, add one solution to the other slowly, and keep concentrations low. Neutralisation releases heat, and concentrated acids and bases can boil and spit.
  • Ammonium nitrate is an oxidiser (GHS H272): keep it away from fuels and heat; anhydrous CaCl₂ gets hot when it dissolves and irritates eyes.
  • A bomb holds about 30 bar of oxygen: only trained users, a checked vessel, never more than about 1 g of sample, and never oily or greasy fittings with oxygen.
What can go wrong
  • Using the peak (or lowest) reading instead of the extrapolated ΔT: the heat leak during mixing is ignored.
  • Hot water cools by about 0.5 K while being poured: Ccal comes out too large (switch the pour loss off to see the true value).
  • Starting the after-line too early, while the temperature is still changing: the extrapolation is biased.
  • A salt that dissolves slowly (anhydrous CaCl₂ forms lumps) spreads the heat over minutes; extrapolation still recovers most of it.
  • In the bomb: forgetting the fuse-wire energy, or using an uncorrected rise.
Data analysis

Export the readings of each run (time_s, T_C) and fit the two lines in Python (Module 8 and 23 lab folders). q = −(m c + mscs + Ccal) ΔT, ΔH = q/n. Propagate the uncertainty of each line at the mixing time into ΔT, then into ΔH.
