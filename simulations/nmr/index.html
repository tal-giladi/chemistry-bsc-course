How to use this, and why

The procedure
  1. Dissolve 5–20 mg of sample in about 0.6 mL of deuterated solvent (CDCl₃) with a little TMS, filter it into a clean 5 mm tube to a depth of about 4 cm.
  2. Insert the tube, lock on the deuterium signal of the solvent and shim the field until the lines are narrow.
  3. Pulse, record the free induction decay, average scans and Fourier-transform it into the spectrum (lesson 14.5).
  4. Reference TMS to 0 ppm, phase and integrate.
  5. Read every signal: shift δ, integral, multiplicity and J; then assign.
Why each step is done
  • Deuterated solvent: its own protons would swamp the sample's; the deuterium signal keeps the field locked.
  • δ = (ν − νTMS)/νspectrometer × 10⁶ is the same on every instrument; J in Hz is the same at every field, so multiplets get narrower in ppm as the field rises.
  • Shimming makes the field the same across the sample: poor shimming broadens every line and hides small couplings.
  • More scans improve the signal-to-noise ratio as √N, which matters for the small outer lines of a septet.
  • n equivalent neighbours give n+1 lines with Pascal heights only when Δν/J is above about 10; below that the exact spin Hamiltonian, which this simulation solves, gives roofing and extra lines.
Safety reasoning

The magnet is always on: steel tools, gas cylinders and chairs can fly into it, and the stray field affects pacemakers and erases cards, so there is a 5-gauss line nobody with an implant crosses. A quench boils off liquid helium, which can displace oxygen in the room. CDCl₃ is harmful if swallowed or inhaled and is a suspected carcinogen (chloroform): prepare samples in a fume hood with gloves. NMR tubes are thin glass and cut hands when they break.

What can go wrong
  • Counting the protons of the signal itself in n+1: a CH₂ next to a CH₃ is a quartet.
  • Reading integrals as absolute numbers: they are ratios; scale them to the formula.
  • Taking the CHCl₃ peak at 7.26 for an aromatic proton, or the water peak at 1.56 for an alkyl group.
  • Missing the outer lines of a septet in the noise and calling it a quintet.
  • Treating a second-order multiplet as first order: if the lines lean towards a partner and the count is wrong, compare Δν with J.
  • Expecting OH and NH protons to split their neighbours: they exchange, so they are broad and unsplit.
Data analysis

Export the peak list and take differences in Hz between neighbouring lines of a multiplet: their mean is J, and the scatter (with the digital resolution, about a quarter of the line width) is its uncertainty. Partners share J within that uncertainty. Export the integrals, divide by the smallest and scale to the formula from lesson 17.1; integrals carry a few per cent of error from noise and baseline, so round to whole protons only when the formula agrees.
