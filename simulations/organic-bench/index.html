How to use this, and why

The procedures
  • Reflux: stirrer or chips first, condenser water in at the bottom, top open, mantle or bath, ring in the lower third.
  • Extraction: identify layers by density and the drop test, vent often, keep every layer; NaHCO₃ pulls carboxylic acids into water (D falls about tenfold per pH unit above the pKa); several small portions beat one large one; brine, then a drying agent until free-flowing.
  • Recrystallisation: minimum boiling solvent, hot filtration if needed, slow cooling, ice-cold wash, dry to constant mass. Best recovery (m − scoldV)/m.
  • Distillation: each boil–condense cycle is one theoretical plate; close boilers need a column and slow heating; bulb level with the side arm; never to dryness.
  • TLC: pencil start line, small spots, closed chamber, co-spot lane, mark the front at once, Rf = spot/front.
  • Melting point: 2–3 mm of dry, fine solid, fast to 15 °C below, then 1–2 °C/min; mixed melting point for identity.
Why each step is done
  • Reflux holds a reaction at a fixed, reproducible temperature for hours without losing solvent; with Ea = 80 kJ/mol the rate rises about 150-fold from 25 to 80 °C.
  • The pH switches benzoic acid between layers: at pH 3 D ≈ 70, at pH 8.3 D ≈ 0.006.
  • Slow crystal growth excludes impurities; a solvent that boils above the compound's melting point makes it oil out.
  • Raoult's law makes the vapour richer in the more volatile liquid, but one stage takes 50 % cyclohexane only to about 71 %.
  • Polar compounds stick to polar silica (low Rf); a more polar eluent moves everything up.
  • An impurity that dissolves in the melt lowers and broadens the melting range (ΔT ≈ RTm²x/ΔfusH).
Safety reasoning

Diethyl ether is extremely flammable (H224) and its heavy vapour creeps to hot plates: no flames, work in the hood. Closed systems under heat or CO₂ build pressure: vent the funnel, keep the reflux open. Superheated liquids bump: chips or stirring before heating, never into a hot liquid. Distilling to dryness overheats residues and concentrates peroxides. Concentrated acids and bases burn (H314). None of this can be done at home; the home activities in Module 24 use only kitchen materials.

What can go wrong
  • Bumping, solvent boiling out of the condenser, a stopper blown out of a funnel.
  • Throwing away the layer that holds the product; an emulsion from vigorous shaking with base (fix: wait, swirl, brine).
  • A wet product from stopping the drying agent while it still clumps.
  • Too much recrystallisation solvent (low recovery), crash cooling (impure), oiling out.
  • A simple distillation for liquids 30 °C apart; a thermometer in the wrong place.
  • Overloaded TLC spots that streak; a front that was never marked; heating a melting point too fast.

    How to use this, and why

    The procedure

    Read the introduction and the reagent cards, and write the amounts and hazards in your notebook. Then take the steps in order: each offers the choices a student makes at the bench. 'Check the product now' gives the mass and yield, the melting range, chemical tests, a TLC with a co-spot of the starting material, and the IR and ¹H NMR key peaks of what you actually have (product plus whatever came with it). Save each check to the runs table, then repeat with deliberate mistakes.

    Why each step is done
    • Excess of the cheap reagent pushes the reaction to completion; the limiting reagent sets the theoretical yield.
    • Quenching, washing and drying each remove one kind of impurity: excess reagent, acids or salts, water.
    • Recrystallisation trades yield for purity; only a pure, dry product gives an honest yield.
    • FeCl₃ answers "is the phenol gone?", the melting point "is it pure and dry?", IR and NMR "did the right bond form?".
    • In a sequence, every intermediate is checked before it is used: impurities travel and multiply.
    Safety reasoning

    Acetic anhydride is flammable and corrosive and its vapour attacks eyes and lungs; concentrated sulfuric and nitric acids cause severe burns, and nitric acid releases toxic NO₂; diethyl ether is extremely flammable; bromobenzene is toxic if inhaled; magnesium releases hydrogen with water; NaOH and KOH are corrosive. Every synthesis here needs a fume hood, goggles, gloves and supervised waste disposal. None of them can be done at home.

    What can go wrong
    • Water in the reagents or glassware: destroyed anhydride or Grignard reagent, low yield.
    • Overheating: by-products, elimination, colour; bumping without boiling chips.
    • Keeping the wrong layer (the 1-bromobutane layer switches between washes).
    • Filtering warm, washing with warm solvent: product lost in the filtrate.
    • Weighing wet: yield over 100 % and a low melting point. Carrying an impure intermediate forward.
    • Wrong stoichiometry in the aldol: the mono product (1 : 1) or an oily excess of benzaldehyde (3 : 1).
    Data analysis

    Theoretical yield = moles of limiting reagent × M(product) (× ½ for the benzoin condensation); percent yield = mass/theoretical; atom economy = M(product)/ΣM(reactants). Export the runs table and use labs/module-24/lesson04_aspirin.py, lesson05_reactions.py and lesson06_practical.py. Quantify a contaminant from NMR integrals by scaling to a group both compounds share (for benzil and benzoin the 10 aromatic H): the 5.95 ppm integral is then the mole fraction of benzoin. Masses carry about ±0.01 g; a melting range wider than 2 °C or ending more than 2 °C low means impure.
