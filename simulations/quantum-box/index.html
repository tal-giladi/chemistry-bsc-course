Convergence: error of E₁ against grid spacing

How to use this, and why

The method
  1. Put ψ on a grid of points spaced Δx apart; the second derivative becomes (ψi+1 − 2ψi + ψi−1)/Δx².
  2. The Schrödinger equation becomes a tridiagonal matrix H with 2t + V(xi) on the diagonal and −t beside it, t = ħ²/(2mΔx²).
  3. The eigenvalues of H are the energies, the eigenvectors (divided by √Δx) the normalised wavefunctions. Points outside the grid are ψ = 0: an infinite wall.
  4. Check against the exact En = n²h²/(8mL²), then use the same solver where no formula exists.
Why each step is done
  • Quantisation is not put in by hand: only certain E give a ψ that fits the walls.
  • The central-difference error shrinks as Δx²: halve the spacing and the energy error drops about four times. Higher states curve more and need more points per half-wave.
  • A finite wall lets ψ leak in as e−κx; the levels drop below the infinite-box values and only a few states stay bound.
Safety reasoning

Nothing hazardous: this is computation. The dyes that the box describes are lab reagents (see the Dye series tab).

What can go wrong
  • Too few grid points: set 25 and compare the error of n = 5 with that of n = 1.
  • A finite well on a grid that stops at the wall: the wavefunction is forced to zero there and the energies come out too high. The solver here extends the grid 1 nm into each wall.
  • Forgetting that there is no n = 0: the lowest energy is above the bottom of the box.
  • Reading ψ instead of ψ² as a probability.
Data analysis

How to use this, and why

The method
  1. Inside a barrier higher than E the wave decays as e−κx, κ = √(2m(V₀ − E))/ħ, and |ψ|² as e−2κx: a straight line on the log plot.
  2. Matching ψ and its slope at both edges gives T = [1 + V₀² sinh²(κd)/(4E(V₀ − E))]⁻¹; for κd ≫ 1, T ≈ 16(E/V₀)(1 − E/V₀)e−2κd.
  3. For a double well, solve the same finite-difference matrix as in the box: the two lowest states are a symmetric and an antisymmetric pair, split by Δ.
Why each step is done
  • The exponent grows with the width, with √m and with √(V₀ − E): doubling the mass is like making the barrier 41 % wider.
  • The antisymmetric state must pass through zero at the barrier, so it curves more and lies higher; a molecule started in one well flips at the frequency Δ/h.
  • Comparing H and D (or NH₃ and ND₃) separates tunnelling from zero-point effects: tunnelling makes the isotope ratio huge.
Safety reasoning

Nothing hazardous here. Ammonia itself is toxic and corrosive (gas, and concentrated solutions); the umbrella model needs no sample.

What can go wrong
  • Using V₀ instead of V₀ − E in κ.
  • Forgetting the factor 2 in e−2κd: answers off by orders of magnitude.
  • Using the thick-barrier formula when κd < 1: compare the two columns at d = 0.1 nm.
  • Thinking the transmitted particle lost energy: E is the same on both sides; only the amplitude shrinks.
  • The double-well splitting depends exponentially on a and Vb: change a from 0.40 to 0.38 Å and watch Δ.
Data analysis

Record T for several widths and plot ln T against d: the slope is −2κ. lesson03_tunnel.py gives 0.777, 0.169, 0.0235 and 1.42 × 10⁻⁴ for an electron with E = 1 eV, V₀ = 2 eV and d = 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0 nm.

How to use this, and why

The method
  1. Harmonic: V = ½kx² with k from ωe; levels (v + ½)ωe, equally spaced.
  2. Morse: V = De(1 − e−ax)² with De = ωe²/(4ωexe) and k = 2Dea²; levels ωe(v + ½) − ωexe(v + ½)².
  3. Both are solved numerically with the same finite-difference matrix as the box, in cm⁻¹ and Å with ħ²/(2u) = 16.858 cm⁻¹ Å².
Why each step is done
  • The fundamental is ωe − 2ωexe, not ωe: compare the gap from v = 0 to 1 with ωe.
  • Isotopes keep the potential (Born–Oppenheimer): ωe ∝ μ−½ and ωexe ∝ μ−1. D³⁵Cl's zero-point energy is about 416 cm⁻¹ lower.
  • ⟨r⟩ grows with v in the Morse well: that is why r₀ from B₀ is longer than re.
Safety reasoning

Nothing hazardous here. HCl gas is corrosive and toxic; in a real spectroscopy lab it lives in a sealed gas cell filled in a fume hood.

What can go wrong
  • Putting v = 0 at the bottom of the well (forgetting the zero-point energy).
  • Predicting the overtone as twice the fundamental.
  • Scaling ωexe by μ−½ for an isotope.
  • Treating the Morse De as the true dissociation energy: it overestimates HCl's D₀ (4.43 eV measured) by about 14 %.
Data analysis

Export the level table and compare with lesson04_vib.py: Morse levels 1482.3, 4367.5, 7147.2, 9821.2 cm⁻¹ for H³⁵Cl, fundamental 2885.3 and overtone 5664.9 cm⁻¹.

How to use this, and why

The method
  1. Levels F(J) = BJ(J + 1) − DJ²(J + 1)², each 2J + 1 times degenerate; lines at 2B(J + 1) − 4D(J + 1)³.
  2. Populations NJ ∝ (2J + 1) e−F(J)hc/kBT, normalised by their sum (the partition function).
  3. Bond length from B: I = h/(8π²cB), r = √(I/μ).
  4. Hydrogen: u = rR obeys −½u″ + [ℓ(ℓ + 1)/2r² − 1/r]u = Eu in atomic units, solved with the same matrix method.
Why each step is done
  • The degeneracy grows with J and the Boltzmann factor shrinks, so the population peaks near J ≈ √(kBT/2hcB) − ½, not at J = 0.
  • The centrifugal barrier ℓ(ℓ + 1)/2r² keeps p and d electrons away from the nucleus: compare the 3s and 3d curves near r = 0.
  • In a pure 1/r potential 2s and 2p have the same energy; in many-electron atoms they do not.
Safety reasoning

Nothing hazardous here. Far-infrared and microwave spectrometers use sealed gas cells; HCl and CO are toxic gases handled in a hood.

What can go wrong
  • Leaving out 2J + 1: J = 0 would always be the most populated level.
  • Writing line positions as BJ(J + 1): those are level energies.
  • c in m/s instead of cm/s with B in cm⁻¹: r off by a factor of 10.
  • Allowing ℓ = n: there is no 1p or 2d.
Data analysis

Export the lines and populations; lesson05_rotor_h.py gives 20.88, 41.74, 62.58, 83.38, 104.13 cm⁻¹ for HCl and the peak at J = 3 at 298 K.

How to use this, and why

Procedure (the real experiment and the model)
  1. Make a 10⁻⁴ mol/L stock of each dye in methanol and dilute to a peak absorbance of 0.3–1 in a 1 cm cell (about 5 × 10⁻⁶ mol/L).
  2. Record against a methanol blank from 400 to 800 nm.
  3. Take λmax at the top of the main band, not at the vibronic shoulder about 1200 cm⁻¹ to the blue.
  4. Dilute twice more: if the band shape changes, aggregates are distorting it.
  5. Model: N π electrons fill levels 1 … N/2; the HOMO → LUMO jump gives λ = 8mecL²/(h(N + 1)) with L = (N + α)ℓ.
  6. Calibrate on one dye, fit two parameters to three, then predict a dye you did not fit.
Why each step is done
  • A cyanine has two equal resonance structures, so its bonds are equalised and the π electrons see a flat box.
  • A polyene's bonds really alternate; the ripple opens a gap V₀(1 − 1/N) that does not close as the chain grows, so the colour converges.
  • Two parameters and three points leave one degree of freedom: a small residual is expected, and the strong ℓ–α correlation says the data cannot separate them. Only a blind prediction tests the model.
Safety reasoning
  • Cyanine dyes stain intensely and are treated as toxic irritants: gloves, small amounts.
  • Methanol is highly flammable and toxic by ingestion, skin contact and inhalation (GHS H225, H301 + H311 + H331, H370): fume hood, no flames, gloves.
  • Neither belongs in a kitchen; the home extension uses carrots, tomato paste and cooking oil only.
What can go wrong
  • Counting the box from nitrogen to nitrogen only: Kuhn's box runs about one bond further at each end, more with big end groups.
  • Using N = number of double bonds instead of the number of π electrons.
  • Calling a two-parameter fit to three points a confirmation.
  • Reading λmax at a shoulder or at an aggregate band from a concentrated solution.
Data analysis

The fit is least squares on ℓ and α with the parameter covariance scaled by the residual variance (one degree of freedom), as curve_fit does by default in lesson04_dyes.py. Export the series table for your report.
