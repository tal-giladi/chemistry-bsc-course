How to use this, and why

Procedure
  1. Rinse the burette twice with a few mL of titrant, fill it above zero, open the tap briefly to clear the air bubble from the tip.
  2. Pipette the sample into the flask, add the indicator (or put in the electrode) and read the burette at eye level: the initial reading.
  3. Add titrant quickly while the colour change is far away, then in 0.1 mL steps, then drop by drop with swirling. Where each drop lands the colour flashes and fades: that warns you the end point is near.
  4. Stop at the first colour change that lasts 30 s. Read the burette again at eye level, to two decimals. The volume is the difference.
  5. With an electrode, log a reading after every addition and take small, even steps (0.05–0.1 mL) through the jump; find the end point from the derivatives or a Gran plot.
  6. Repeat until three titrations agree; run a blank where an indicator needs an excess of titrant (Mohr).
Why each step is done
  • Water left in an unrinsed burette dilutes the titrant, so every titration needs more of it and every result comes out high.
  • An air bubble that escapes from the tip counts as delivered volume although no titrant reached the flask.
  • The scale is on the front of the glass and the meniscus in the middle of the tube: only a sight line level with it gives the right mark (parallax).
  • An indicator is a weak acid (or a redox couple, or a metal complex) that changes over about pKIn ± 1. Its window must fall inside the steep jump of the curve, or the end point is far from equivalence.
  • Mohr needs pH 7–10: in acid chromate becomes HCrO₄⁻ and the red Ag₂CrO₄ appears late; above pH 10 silver oxide precipitates. The visible colour always needs some excess silver, so a blank is subtracted.
  • EDTA binds metals only as Y⁴⁻, whose share falls steeply below pH 10; the conditional constant Kf′ = αY⁴⁻Kf decides whether the end point is sharp. At pH 12 Mg(OH)₂ precipitates and only Ca is titrated.
  • A pH electrode has its own offset and slope; two buffers measure both. An aged electrode is slower and its slope is lower.
Safety reasoning
  • 0.1 M NaOH and HCl are irritant to eyes: safety glasses always. Fill the burette below eye level, with a funnel, and remove the funnel before titrating.
  • Cerium(IV) solutions are in 1 M sulfuric acid: corrosive. Concentrated acids for dissolving steel need a fume hood.
  • Silver nitrate stains skin and is toxic to aquatic life; potassium chromate is a carcinogen (GHS H350): gloves, small amounts, collect the waste, never down the sink.
  • The ammonia buffer for EDTA titrations is irritant and smells: use it in a hood or a well-ventilated room.
  • That is why most of these titrations are only simulated in this course; the home titration of vinegar uses baking soda and red cabbage.
What can go wrong
  • Overshooting: a 1 mL addition, or a tap closed too late, carries you past the end point; one drop is 0.05 mL, about 0.2 % of 25 mL.
  • Choosing the indicator by habit: methyl red with acetic acid stops about a third of the way.
  • Reading from above or below the meniscus, and not the same way each time.
  • Reading an electrode before it settles: the curve lags behind the titrant and the end point comes late.
  • An uncalibrated electrode: the end-point volume is still right, but every pH, and so the pKa, is off.
  • Mohr in acid, or without a blank; EDTA at pH 8, where Mg barely reacts.
Data analysis

Export the readings (burette reading in mL and pH, mV or pAg) and analyse them in Python (Module 9 and 10 lab folders): first-derivative peak, second-derivative zero crossing and, for a weak acid, a Gran plot of V·10−pH against V over 50–95 % of the way. For visual titrations, report the mean of three or more end points with their standard deviation and the uncertainty budget of a burette volume: tolerance 0.05/√3, two readings of ±0.02 mL and ± half a drop.
