How to use this, and why

Procedure
  1. Absorbance: choose λmax, a quartz cell below 300 nm, a slit narrow compared with the band; zero on a blank in the same cell; read standards from low to high; dilute samples into the range of the standards, ideally near A = 0.4.
  2. Mixtures: record the pure spectra, pick two wavelengths where each dye absorbs strongly and the other weakly, calibrate both dyes at both wavelengths, then solve the 2 × 2 system or fit the whole spectrum.
  3. Equilibrium constant: standards with a large excess of Fe³⁺ give εb; mixtures with comparable concentrations give K through an ICE table; correct εb for incomplete complexation and iterate; check the 1:1 model with a Job plot.
  4. Fluorescence: excite at 350 nm, read at 450 nm at 90°, in sulfuric acid; dilute until the signal scales with the dilution; calibrate externally and by standard addition.
  5. Flame AAS: warm the lamp, set wavelength and slit, light the flame, optimise the burner height, zero on the blank, standards low to high, samples, then check standards, a spike and a duplicate.
Why each step is done
  • A = −log₁₀T = εbc only for dilute solutions and monochromatic light; stray light s caps the reading at −log₁₀[s/(1 + s)] and bends the top of the calibration.
  • The relative error of c is smallest near A = 0.43; at low A two nearly equal signals are subtracted, at high A almost no light arrives.
  • Absorbances add, so n absorbers need n well-chosen wavelengths; a full-spectrum fit averages the noise of all of them, and its residuals reveal an absorber that is not in the model.
  • "Almost all" SCN⁻ complexed is not all: with K[Fe]₀ = 28 the standards are 96.6 % complexed, and K comes out several per cent high unless corrected.
  • Concentrated quinine absorbs its own excitation light before the centre of the cell (inner filter); chloride quenches it (Stern–Volmer, KSV ≈ 130 L/mol).
  • In the flame, phosphate and sulfate hold calcium in compounds that do not atomise; excess La releases it. In a hot flame, Na and Ca ionise; excess K suppresses that. A deuterium lamp removes broad background absorption.
Safety reasoning
  • Iron stock in nitric acid is corrosive: gloves and goggles. Potassium thiocyanate is harmful (H302, H312, H332) and very toxic to aquatic life (H400): collect all waste.
  • Quinine is harmful if swallowed and may cause skin allergy (H302, H317): gloves for the standards. Never look into a UV lamp or its reflection.
  • Acetylene can decompose explosively above about 1 bar gauge: keep the regulator low and the cylinder chained upright. The AAS burner sits under extraction (UV, metal vapours); the drain trap must be full to stop a flashback; nitrous oxide flames are lit and put out through air–acetylene.
  • Quartz cuvettes are expensive and fragile; handle them by the frosted sides.
What can go wrong
  • Reading a sample above the top standard, or a plastic cell in the UV; forgetting to zero on the blank.
  • Reading each dye at its own peak and ignoring the other one; choosing wavelengths where the two spectra look alike.
  • Blanking the FeSCN²⁺ standards with water; Fe³⁺ without acid (hydrolysis to FeOH²⁺); too much SCN⁻ (Fe(SCN)₂⁺); reading long after mixing (the colour fades).
  • Undiluted tonic; HCl instead of H₂SO₄; trusting external calibration in a matrix that changes the slope.
  • Skipping the lamp warm-up (drift looks like a changing calibration); La in the samples only; spikes beyond the linear range; reading a salty sample without background correction.
Data analysis

Export the data of a tab (CSV) and analyse it in Python as in the Module 19, 23 and 25 lab folders: straight-line calibration with residuals and Harvey's sc (eq. 5.12), the stray-light model fitted over the whole range, the 2 × 2 system and classical least squares for mixtures, the ICE table with the iterated εb correction and a 95 % interval for K, Stern–Volmer and standard addition with the extrapolation uncertainty, and for AAS the linear range, LOD, spike recovery and check-standard drift.
