How to use this, and why

Procedure
  1. Measure each resistor alone on the Ω range, before it goes on the board.
  2. Build the network with the battery disconnected and measure the resistance between the two points where the battery will connect: R1 + R2 ∥ (R3 + R4).
  3. Connect the battery. Set DC volts, black probe on battery minus and leave it there.
  4. Read the battery voltage at its terminals with the network connected, then VA and VB.
  5. Check Kirchhoff's voltage law: the voltages across R1 and across R2 add up to Vs.
  6. Predict VA and VB from your measured resistances and Vs, and compare.
Why each step is done
  • Resistors differ from their printed value by up to their tolerance (gold band, 5 %); predictions from measured values agree with readings to about 1 %.
  • An ohmmeter drives its own small current; any other current in the circuit spoils the reading, so resistance is measured unpowered.
  • A voltmeter goes across a component and has about 10 MΩ inside, so it barely disturbs the circuit. Compute currents from voltages with Ohm's law.
  • The battery voltage is read with the load connected because a cell's internal resistance makes it sag under load.
  • Writing the current law at nodes A and B gives two linear equations: a 2 × 2 matrix problem.
Safety reasoning
  • AA or AAA alkaline cells in a holder only: never mains sockets, chargers, power banks, lithium-ion packs or coin cells (a swallowed coin cell burns through tissue).
  • Never join the battery terminals with a wire or with the meter on its current range: that is a short circuit; the wire gets hot and the cell can leak. Try it here with the mA function across the battery.
  • Every resistor here dissipates well under its 0.25 W rating, so nothing gets warm.
What can go wrong
  • A meter on the current range across a battery or a resistor shorts it (this one blows its fuse).
  • Measuring resistance in a powered circuit gives a meaningless number.
  • Swapped probes give a negative reading; the size is still right.
  • A tired battery reads fine with nothing connected and sags under load: compare the terminal voltage with the battery connected and disconnected.
  • Predicting from printed values instead of measured ones: differences up to 5 % are tolerance, not a broken law.
  • A very large resistor network: the 10 MΩ meter starts to load it. Set R4 to 2 MΩ and read VB.
Data analysis

The network file has the format of network_example.csv (Module 4 lab folder): the battery voltage, the four measured resistances and VA, VB, from your latest matching readings. lesson04_network.py solves the node equations from them. A 3½-digit meter is good to about ±(0.5 % + 2 digits).

Not trained here: plugging parts into the right breadboard rows, holding probes steady, and reading resistor colour bands.

How to use this, and why

Procedure
  1. With nothing connected, measure the resistor and the coil on the ohmmeter.
  2. Build tip → inductor → capacitor → resistor → sleeve, with the resistor at the sleeve (ground) end.
  3. Turn the phone volume to about a third, start a single sine tone, then plug in.
  4. On AC volts, adjust the volume so that Vin stays under 200 mV at every frequency (check 300 Hz), and fix the meter on the 200 mV range.
  5. At each frequency read Vin and then VR. Put most points between 0.7 f₀ and 1.4 f₀.
  6. Fit H = A/√(1 + Q²(f/f₀ − f₀/f)²); get Rtot = R/A, L = QRtot/2πf₀, C = 1/(2πf₀QRtot).
  7. Swap the 10 Ω resistor for 47 Ω, predict Q first, and check at a few frequencies.
Why each step is done
  • The phone's output changes with frequency and load, and a cheap meter reads AC less accurately at high frequency. Both multiply Vin and VR by the same factor on one range, so the ratio cancels them.
  • At f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)) the reactances cancel and |Z| = Rtot, so the current is largest; the half-power width is f₀/Q.
  • The circuit equation L q̈ + R q̇ + q/C = V is the driven spring: L is the mass, R the friction, 1/C the stiffness.
  • The ohmmeter's coil reading is a DC value; a coil loses a little more at kilohertz, so the fitted Rtot comes out a few ohms higher.
Safety reasoning
  • Battery or USB power only: a phone on its own battery, never a mains adapter, mains amplifier or speaker system.
  • No earphones while the tone runs, and volume down before plugging in: a full-volume tone can damage hearing.
  • Never join tip and sleeve directly; the series resistor protects the phone's output. Use a film or ceramic capacitor, not an electrolytic one.
  • Measure resistance only with the plug pulled out.
What can go wrong
  • Letting the meter auto-range: Vin and VR are read on different ranges with different frequency responses, and the ratio no longer cancels them.
  • Too much volume: the 200 mV range overloads ("1" on the left of the display) at low frequency.
  • Ignoring the coil's resistance: with only the 10 Ω resistor in Rtot the predicted Q is 10, four times too high.
  • No peak at all: the capacitor is open (wrong row). VR almost equal to Vin everywhere: something is shorted. A peak near 5 kHz: a 0.1 µF capacitor.
  • Readings jumpy above about 3 kHz: cheap meters are only specified for AC up to a few hundred hertz or 1 kHz.
  • A linear grid of frequencies wastes points far from the peak.
Data analysis

Export the readings as f_Hz,Vin_mV,VR_mV (the format of rlc_example.csv) and run lesson06_rlc.py with your ohmmeter values in R_OHM and R_COIL_DC. The fit here uses the same weights as the lab script: u(H) = H√2 × 0.5 % combined with one count (0.1 mV) on VR, and parameter uncertainties from the weighted covariance; χ² per degree of freedom near 1 means the 0.5 % was honest.

Not trained here: breadboarding, soldering a 3.5 mm plug, and using an oscilloscope to see the phase swing from +90° to −90° across the resonance.

Model: DC network solved by nodal analysis with the meter as part of the circuit (10 MΩ voltmeter, about 1 Ω on the current range, fuse at 200 mA); 3½-digit display with ±(0.5 % + 2 digits)-sized errors. AC: series impedance Z = R + Rcoil + Rcore(f) + j(ωL − 1/ωC) driven by a phone output of about 10 Ω, a meter whose AC response falls off differently on each range, 0.5 % reading noise rising above 3 kHz. Cell data: fresh AA alkaline about 1.58 V and 0.15 Ω per cell (typical datasheet values). Constants: none beyond 2π.
