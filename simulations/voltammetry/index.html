How to use this, and why

Procedure
  1. Polish the working electrode, check the reference, add 0.1–0.5 M supporting electrolyte and purge with N₂ for 10 min.
  2. Run a blank of the electrolyte alone: it shows the usable window and the charging background.
  3. Start where nothing reacts and switch 150–200 mV past the peak, so the curve has time to decay.
  4. Run a known couple first (ferricyanide): if its ΔEp is far above 60–70 mV, repolish.
  5. Record a scan-rate series (20 mV/s to 1 V/s). Plot ip against √v and against v; read ΔEp and E1/2 at each rate.
  6. Change the concentration once: an iR shift changes with it, a kinetic separation does not.
  7. For a polarisation curve, sweep slowly from the anodic to the cathodic side; fit Tafel lines more than about 100 mV from equilibrium and below the limiting current, after correcting for Ru.
Why each step is done
  • A reference that carries no current keeps its potential; the potentiostat sets the working electrode against it and passes the current through the counter electrode.
  • Excess supporting electrolyte carries the current, so the analyte moves only by diffusion, and keeps Ru low.
  • Dissolved O₂ (about 0.26 mM in air-saturated water) is reduced at negative potentials and adds its own large wave.
  • A fouled surface slows electron transfer: the peaks move apart and look like a slow couple.
  • ip ∝ √v means diffusion; ip ∝ v means the species sits on the surface; the charging current also grows as v, so fast scans lose the faradaic signal in the background.
  • Nicholson's ψ = k⁰/√(πDnFv/RT) links ΔEp to k⁰, but only between about 63 and 150 mV and only with an independent D.
  • Butler–Volmer: i = i₀[eαafη − e−αcfη]; near η = 0 the electrode is a resistor Rct = RT/(Fi₀); far from it a Tafel line of slope 2.303RT/(αF).
Safety reasoning
  • Potentials and currents in CV are harmless; the chemicals are not always. Potassium ferricyanide is low-hazard but must not meet strong acid (hydrogen cyanide can form).
  • Alumina polishing slurry is a fine dust when dry: keep it wet.
  • Perchlorate electrolytes for organic solvents are oxidisers and can explode when dry with organic material: never scrape dry residues.
  • Gas cylinders (N₂, Ar) are chained upright; hydrogen evolution experiments in sulfuric acid need goggles and a vented cell.
What can go wrong
  • Oxygen left in the cell: an extra wave near −0.2 to −0.4 V that grows with every scan.
  • No supporting electrolyte or a blocked reference frit: large Ru, peaks pulled apart, distorted shapes, drifting potentials.
  • An unpolished electrode read as a slow couple; Ru read as slow kinetics (test: halve the concentration).
  • Measuring the return peak from zero current instead of from the decaying forward current.
  • Getting D from fast scans of a slow couple: Randles–Ševčík then underestimates it, and k⁰ from that D is wrong too.
  • Fitting a Tafel line too close to η = 0, into the iR-bent part, or into the mass-transport plateau: the slope comes out too large.
Data analysis

Export the data of a tab (CSV) and analyse them in Python as in the Module 19, 25 and 32 lab folders: peak table, ip against √v through the origin and D from the slope (Randles–Ševčík, ip = 2.69 × 10⁵ n3/2AD1/2Cv1/2), the log–log slope, ψ and k⁰ for each scan with ΔEp ≤ 150 mV, and for polarisation curves the iR correction, a linear fit of log|i| against η for b and log i₀, and the micro-polarisation slope for Rct. Uncertainties come from the scatter of the fit (the tables here give the standard error).
