# Instructor notes — 11.1 Reading a research paper efficiently

- **Objective:** read a paper in three passes; answer the five Cs after a 10-minute first pass; read figures and tables as the evidence; check a paper against its own numbers and criteria; write a 150-word structured summary in one's own words.
- **Likely confusion:** learners think "efficient" means skimming everything once. The point is a decision after pass 1 and depth only where it pays. Also: what the "±" in a table means (SD, SE or CI) is often only in a footnote.
- **Misconception:** "peer-reviewed means correct". Reviewers rarely see raw data and never repeat the work; tables still contain arithmetic slips (Jacinto Table 4, M5).
- **Analogy:** a paper is a lawyer's case. The abstract is the opening speech, the figures and tables are the exhibits, and the reader is the judge who checks whether the exhibits prove what the speech claims.
- **Expected hands-on result:** claim names tartrazine, soda drinks, MIP colour change read by phone, UHPLC reference. Numbers: LOD 1.2 mg/L, range 0–20 mg/L, RSD 3.3–5.3 % (n = 10), five drinks × 3. Recomputed Table 4: +6.3, −10.3, +16.7, +7.7, +11.1 % (printed 15.9 for M5). Missing: spike recovery, CRM, other yellow dyes (selectivity from an earlier study), data not shared.
- **Extension:** do a third pass on the Coutinho et al. ACS Omega paper and write a one-paragraph referee comment; or compare the two papers' validation steps in a table (recovery, reference method, n, phones tested, data availability).
- **Scope:** both papers were checked on 2026-10-17 (CC BY 4.0). The point is reading, not judging the authors; the discrepancies found are small and do not overturn either paper's main claim.
