# Instructor notes — 28.1 Potential energy surfaces and reaction coordinates

- Objective: the learner classifies stationary points with the Hessian, traces a minimum-energy path, reads multistep profiles (rate-determining TS measured from the lowest preceding state), converts barriers to rates with Eyring, and applies kinetic/thermodynamic control and Curtin–Hammett.
- Likely confusion: intermediate versus transition state (well versus pass); potential energy surface versus Gibbs energy profile; why a minimiser cannot find a saddle.
- Misconception: "the step with the largest individual barrier is rate-determining". Part B question 2 (TS2 at +85 above reactants, only 45 above the intermediate) is built to break it. Second: "the major conformer gives the major product" (Curtin–Hammett worked example 2).
- Analogy: a hiker in fog crossing a mountain range: valleys are molecules, the lowest pass on the ridge is the transition state, and the hiker's trail is the reaction coordinate. A straight line between two valleys goes over a summit.
- Expected hands-on result: Müller–Brown minima (−0.558, 1.442) −146.70, (0.623, 0.028) −108.17, (−0.050, 0.467) −80.77; saddles (−0.822, 0.624) −40.66 and (0.212, 0.293) −72.25 with negative eigenvalues about −751 and −735; barriers 106.0 (A → C) and 8.5 (C → B); straight line A → B peaks 159 above A. Worked: half-lives 11.6 s, 10 h, 3.7 y for 80/100/120 kJ/mol; Curtin–Hammett 2.24 (69 % from the 17 % conformer). Pencil: one imaginary frequency → IRC; step 2, 85 kJ/mol, k = 7.9e-3 s⁻¹ (t½ about 90 s); 40:1 kinetic, 22:1 thermodynamic.
- Extension: add a nudged elastic band (straight-line guess relaxed perpendicular to the path) to find the MEP without knowing the saddle first, and compare with the eigenvector route; or estimate the Eyring rate of each Müller–Brown step reading the unit as kJ/mol.
