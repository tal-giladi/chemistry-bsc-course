# 18.3 · Coordination compounds: geometry and isomers

A coordination compound is a metal atom or ion surrounded by molecules or ions that each donate a pair of electrons to it. Haemoglobin, the anticancer drug cisplatin, the pigment phthalocyanine blue and the iron in a pharmacy supplement tablet are all coordination chemistry. This lesson gives you the vocabulary and the bookkeeping: how to read a formula, find the metal's oxidation state and d-electron count, name the compound, predict its shape and count its isomers. You end with a home experiment in which one ligand pushes another off an iron ion.

## Why it matters

Cisplatin, cis-[Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂], is one of the most widely used cancer drugs. Its trans isomer has the same formula and is clinically useless, because only the cis arrangement can bind two neighbouring bases of DNA. A chemist who cannot tell the isomers apart, or count how many are possible, cannot make or test such a drug. The same skills decide how EDTA in food and medicine traps metal ions, and how your body stores and moves iron.

## Core ideas

> [!NOTE]
> The concepts here (Werner's coordination theory, isomerism, the chelate effect) are stable. Naming follows the IUPAC 2005 recommendations (the Red Book), still current in October 2026. Many textbooks, including OpenStax, still use the older ligand names such as "chloro"; both are understood.

### The parts of a complex

In $[\text{Co(NH}_3)_5\text{Cl}]\text{Cl}_2$:

- the **central metal** is cobalt;
- the **ligands** are the five NH₃ and one Cl⁻ inside the brackets. A ligand is a Lewis base: it donates an electron pair to the metal, which acts as a Lewis acid. The atom that donates is the **donor atom** (N in NH₃);
- the brackets enclose the **coordination sphere**; the two Cl⁻ outside are **counter-ions** that balance the charge;
- the **coordination number** (CN) is the number of donor atoms bound to the metal: here 6.

A ligand with one donor atom is **monodentate** (NH₃, H₂O, Cl⁻, CN⁻). One with two is **bidentate**: ethylenediamine, H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂ ("en"), and oxalate, C₂O₄²⁻ ("ox"). EDTA⁴⁻ is **hexadentate**. A ligand that binds through two or more atoms forms a ring with the metal and is called a **chelating** ligand (from the Greek for claw).

### Oxidation state and d count

Find the metal's **oxidation state** from the charges: the complex charge equals the metal's oxidation state plus the sum of the ligand charges. In $[\text{Co(NH}_3)_5\text{Cl}]^{2+}$, NH₃ is neutral and Cl⁻ is −1, so Co is +3.

Then the **d-electron count**: group number minus oxidation state. Cobalt is in group 9, so Co(III) is d⁶. This works because, as lesson [18.1](lesson-01.md) showed, the ion has lost its 4s electrons first and all the rest are in 3d. The d count drives everything in lessons 18.4 and 18.5: shape preferences, colour and magnetism.

### Geometry

The common coordination numbers and shapes:

| CN | Geometry | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | linear | $[\text{Ag(NH}_3)_2]^+$ |
| 4 | tetrahedral | $[\text{CoCl}_4]^{2-}$ |
| 4 | square planar | $[\text{PtCl}_4]^{2-}$, cisplatin |
| 6 | octahedral | $[\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]^{3+}$, $[\text{Fe(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{2+}$ |

VSEPR (lesson [07.4](../module-07/lesson-04.md)) does not predict transition-metal shapes, because the d electrons are not ordinary lone pairs. Use these rules of thumb instead: six ligands are almost always octahedral; four ligands are tetrahedral, except for d⁸ ions with strong ligands (Pt(II), Pd(II), Au(III), often Ni(II) with CN⁻), which are square planar. Lesson 18.4 explains why.

### Naming (IUPAC 2005)

1. Cation before anion, as for any salt.
2. Inside the complex, ligands in alphabetical order (ignoring multiplying prefixes), then the metal.
3. Anionic ligands end in "-ido" or "-ato": chlorido, cyanido, hydroxido, oxalato. Neutral ligands keep their names, except aqua (H₂O), ammine (NH₃) and carbonyl (CO).
4. Prefixes di-, tri-, tetra-; for ligand names that already contain a number, bis-, tris-, tetrakis-.
5. The metal's oxidation state in Roman numerals. An anionic complex takes "-ate", with the Latin stem for some metals (ferrate, cuprate, argentate, aurate, plumbate).

So $[\text{Co(NH}_3)_5\text{Cl}]\text{Cl}_2$ is pentaamminechloridocobalt(III) chloride, and $\text{K}_4[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]$ is potassium hexacyanidoferrate(II).

### Isomers

Isomers have the same formula but different arrangements.

**Structural isomers** have different bonds:

- **ionisation**: an anion swaps between the coordination sphere and the counter-ion position, as in $[\text{CoBr(NH}_3)_5]\text{SO}_4$ and $[\text{Co(SO}_4)(\text{NH}_3)_5]\text{Br}$. They give different precipitates with Ag⁺ or Ba²⁺;
- **hydrate**: water inside or outside the sphere. "CrCl₃·6H₂O" exists as violet $[\text{Cr(H}_2\text{O)}_6]\text{Cl}_3$ and as green $[\text{CrCl}_2(\text{H}_2\text{O})_4]\text{Cl}\cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$;
- **linkage**: an ambidentate ligand binds through different atoms, such as thiocyanate through S or N, or nitrite through N (nitrito-κN, the yellow "nitro" isomer of $[\text{Co(NH}_3)_5(\text{NO}_2)]^{2+}$) or through O (the red "nitrito" isomer).

**Stereoisomers** have the same bonds in different spatial arrangements:

- **cis/trans** in square planar MA₂B₂ and octahedral MA₄B₂: cis-$[\text{Co(NH}_3)_4\text{Cl}_2]^+$ is violet, trans is green;
- **fac/mer** in octahedral MA₃B₃: the three A ligands on one face (fac) or around a meridian (mer);
- **optical**: mirror images that cannot be superimposed, such as the two forms of $[\text{Co(en)}_3]^{3+}$, called Δ and Λ (a right- and a left-handed propeller).

How many isomers are possible? Place the ligands on the six corners of the octahedron in every possible way, then call two placements the same if a rotation turns one into the other. Count with rotations only and you count mirror images separately; add reflections and each mirror pair merges. That is exactly the program in Part B.

### The chelate effect

A chelating ligand binds far more strongly than the same number of similar monodentate ligands. For nickel(II) in water (LibreTexts):

$$[\text{Ni(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{2+} + 6\,\text{NH}_3 \rightleftharpoons [\text{Ni(NH}_3)_6]^{2+} + 6\,\text{H}_2\text{O} \qquad K = 4\times10^{8}$$

$$[\text{Ni(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{2+} + 3\,\text{en} \rightleftharpoons [\text{Ni(en)}_3]^{2+} + 6\,\text{H}_2\text{O} \qquad K = 2\times10^{18}$$

Both form six Ni–N bonds, so the enthalpy changes are similar. The difference is entropy. Swap the ligands directly: $[\text{Ni(NH}_3)_6]^{2+} + 3\,\text{en} \to [\text{Ni(en)}_3]^{2+} + 6\,\text{NH}_3$ turns 4 particles into 7, and the gain in translational entropy drives it. Once one end of an en is bound, the other end is held close to the metal, so it binds again quickly if it lets go. Five-membered rings (as with en, oxalate and EDTA) are the most stable, because they are almost free of strain.

## Worked example

### 1. Reading K₃[Fe(C₂O₄)₃]

- Charges: three K⁺ outside, so the complex is $[\text{Fe(C}_2\text{O}_4)_3]^{3-}$. Oxalate is −2: $x + 3(-2) = -3$, so iron is +3.
- d count: iron is in group 8, so Fe(III) is d⁵.
- Each oxalate binds through two O atoms: CN = 6, octahedral.
- Name: potassium tris(oxalato)ferrate(III). "Tris" because the ligand name is in brackets; "ferrate" because the complex is an anion.
- Isomers: like $[\text{Co(en)}_3]^{3+}$, three bidentate ligands make a propeller, so it exists as a Δ/Λ pair of enantiomers. It is the green, light-sensitive salt that photochemists use to measure light intensity.

### 2. How much does the chelate effect give?

With $\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$ (lesson [13.4](../module-13/lesson-04.md)) at 298.15 K, $RT$ = 2.479 kJ/mol:

$$\Delta G^\circ(\text{NH}_3) = -2.479 \ln(4\times10^{8}) = -49.1\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

$$\Delta G^\circ(\text{en}) = -2.479 \ln(2\times10^{18}) = -104.5\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

The swap $[\text{Ni(NH}_3)_6]^{2+} + 3\,\text{en} \to [\text{Ni(en)}_3]^{2+} + 6\,\text{NH}_3$ has $K = 2\times10^{18}/4\times10^{8} = 5\times10^{9}$ and $\Delta G^\circ = -55.4$ kJ/mol. In a solution with equal ammonia and en concentrations, nearly every nickel ion ends up chelated. This is why EDTA, with six donor atoms in one molecule, is the standard reagent for titrating metal ions (Module 10) and for treating lead poisoning.

1. Cr(III), d³, CN 6, octahedral; hexaaquachromium(III) chloride.
2. Pt(II), d⁸, CN 4, square planar (d⁸ with a heavy metal); potassium tetrachloridoplatinate(II).
3. Co(III), d⁶, CN 6 (two bidentate en + two Cl⁻), octahedral; dichloridobis(ethylenediamine)cobalt(III) chloride (alphabetical: chlorido before ethylenediamine).
4. Ni(0), d¹⁰, CN 4, tetrahedral; tetracarbonylnickel(0). CO is neutral, so the metal is in oxidation state zero.

6. Three: trans (not chiral, it has mirror planes) and a cis pair, Δ and Λ, which are mirror images.

### Part B — Python: counting isomers by symmetry (20 min)

`inorg18.py` lists the 24 rotations of an octahedron as permutations of the six corners (`octahedral_operations`), and the 48 operations including reflections. Open `lesson03_isomers.py` and write `canonical(placement, ops)`: apply every operation to a placement and return the smallest image, so that all placements of the same isomer share one label. Then run:

```bash
py lesson03_isomers.py
py lesson03_isomers.py A A B B C D
```

How you know it worked: MA₆ 1; MA₅B 1; MA₄B₂ 2 (cis, trans); MA₃B₃ 2 (fac, mer); MA₂B₂C₂ 6 stereoisomers, 5 if mirror images are merged, so one enantiomer pair; M(AA)₃ 2 (one pair); M(AA)₂B₂ 3 (trans and a cis pair); six different ligands 30 stereoisomers, 15 enantiomer pairs. MA₂B₂CD gives 8, with 2 pairs. Check your answers to Part A against the program.

<details><summary>Reference solution</summary>

```python
def canonical(placement, ops):
    images = []
    for op in ops:
        labels, rings = apply(op, placement)
        images.append((labels, tuple(sorted(rings))))
    return min(images)
```

Full code in `lesson03_isomers_solution.py`. `min` works because tuples compare element by element; any rule that picks the same image for every member of a set would do. This is the counting idea of group theory, which Module 20 develops properly: the number of distinct arrangements is set by the symmetry operations that leave the shape unchanged.

</details>

### Part C — kitchen: iron, tea and lemon (25 min)

> [!CAUTION]
> Iron tablets are dangerous to young children: US law requires them to carry the warning that accidental overdose of iron-containing products is a leading cause of fatal poisoning in children under 6. Use one tablet, keep the pack closed and out of reach, and pour all the test liquids down the sink when you finish. Do not drink the tea. Wear safety glasses; handle the just-boiled water carefully.

1. Brew two tea bags in about 300 mL of just-boiled water for 5 minutes, remove the bags and let the tea cool. Pour about 80 mL into each of three cups (A, B, C).
2. Crush the iron tablet in the closed zip bag with the back of a spoon. Tip half the powder into cup B and half into cup C. Stir. Cup A is the reference.
3. Watch for 5 minutes. Photograph the three cups side by side.
4. Add one teaspoon of lemon juice to cup C, stir, and watch for 2 more minutes. Photograph again.
5. Record colours and times on a [notebook page](../../templates/notebook-page.md).

How you know it worked: cups B and C turn dark blue-grey to almost black within a minute or two, much darker than A. After lemon juice, cup C fades back towards a lighter amber-brown. If your tablet is iron bisglycinate ("gentle iron"), the darkening is slower and weaker.

<details><summary>Explaining your result</summary>

Tea contains polyphenols with gallate groups (three neighbouring OH groups on a benzene ring). Iron(II) from the tablet is oxidised to iron(III) by air, and Fe³⁺ binds the deprotonated O atoms as a chelating ligand, giving intensely coloured iron–polyphenol complexes. This is the chemistry of iron gall ink, used for writing for over a thousand years. Lemon juice adds acid, which protonates the O donor atoms so they can no longer bind, and citrate, a chelating ligand of its own, competes for the iron. Ligand substitution and the chelate effect, in a teacup. Bisglycinate iron is already chelated by two glycine ligands, so the tea polyphenols must displace them first. The colour here is a charge-transfer band, not a d–d band (lesson 18.5).

</details>

## Common mistakes

- **Counting ligands instead of donor atoms.** $[\text{Co(en)}_3]^{3+}$ has three ligands but CN 6.
- **Forgetting the counter-ions when finding the oxidation state.** Work out the charge of the bracketed complex first.
- **Using VSEPR for transition metals.** Four-coordinate d⁸ Pt(II) is square planar, not tetrahedral.
- **Calling cis/trans isomers "the same molecule drawn differently".** Check by rotating: if no rotation superimposes them, they are isomers (cis and trans cisplatin act completely differently).
- **Missing the chiral cis isomers.** Any octahedral complex with two or three chelate rings arranged cis is chiral; count its mirror image.

## Key takeaways

- Read a complex in order: charge of the coordination sphere, oxidation state, d count (group − oxidation state), CN, geometry.
- CN 6 is octahedral; CN 4 is tetrahedral except for d⁸ heavy metals, which are square planar.
- Isomers are structural (ionisation, hydrate, linkage) or stereo (cis/trans, fac/mer, Δ/Λ); counting them is a symmetry problem.
- The chelate effect is mostly entropy: $[\text{Ni(en)}_3]^{2+}$ is $5\times10^9$ times more favoured than $[\text{Ni(NH}_3)_6]^{2+}$, 55 kJ/mol in $\Delta G^\circ$.
- In a teacup, polyphenols chelate iron and lemon juice takes it back: ligands compete.
