# Instructor notes — 10.4 Gravimetry, electrogravimetry and coulometry

- Objective: bench routine of precipitation gravimetry and why each step; heating to constant mass with a real kitchen experiment (NaHCO3 → Na2CO3, 36.92 % loss); Faraday's law, current efficiency, electrogravimetry of Cu and coulometric titrations. Covers the S3 experiments "gravimetry" and "electrogravimetry and coulometry".
- Likely confusion: n (electrons per particle) vs n (moles); constant mass vs fixed heating time; why electrogravimetry tolerates <100 % current efficiency and coulometry does not.
- Misconception: "a mass that keeps changing means the scale is broken" — usually temperature and moisture. And "gravimetry is old so it is inaccurate" — it is a primary method.
- Analogy: coulometry as counting coins by weighing the bag versus counting them one by one; Faraday's constant is the exchange rate between coulombs and moles.
- Expected hands-on result: example sample 9.99 g, constant mass 9.85 g at 75 min, loss 36.64 %, apparent purity 99.2 ± 0.1 % (true model 100 %, 0.015 g water pickup); GF 0.2474 → 1413 mg/L; coulometry 5.00e-5 mol; Cu efficiency 99.5 %. Real kitchen runs: 36.3–36.9 %; ovens that run cool need 90+ min.
- Data note: `oven_example.csv` from `make_data.py` (seed 1010), not measured.
- Safety: oven mitts; Na2CO3 is H319 (eye irritant) — glasses; no tasting. No electrolysis at home: salt-water electrolysis makes chlorine, and copper sulfate is a lab reagent.
- Extension: measure the rate of decomposition at 120 °C vs 200 °C (first-order fit), linking to Module 22 kinetics.
