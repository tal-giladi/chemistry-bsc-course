# Instructor notes — 14.1 Measuring a spring with your phone

- Objective: the learner measures one spring's force constant by two independent methods, includes the spring's effective mass, and decides with uncertainties whether the methods agree.
- Likely confusion: which mass goes on the x-axis (total hanging mass, including bag and string); why the $T^2$ line has a negative mass-axis crossing; reading the period from phyphox (autocorrelation) rather than counting bounces.
- Misconception: "The static and dynamic constants are different physical quantities." They are the same $k$; a gap means a missing effect (spring mass, initial tension, overstretch) or a systematic error.
- Analogy: a crowd pushing a bus. The people at the back barely move, those at the front move with the bus; on average only part of the crowd's mass is moving with it, just as only a third of the spring's mass counts.
- Expected hands-on result: example data give $k$ = 24.06 ± 0.20 N/m (static), 24.03 ± 0.11 N/m (dynamic), difference 0.1 $u_c$, $m_\text{eff}$ = 10.7 ± 1.5 g against $m_s/3$ = 10 g; 300 g gives $f$ = 1.42 Hz. Real springs: agreement within 1–3 %; $m_\text{eff}$ between 0.25 and 0.5 $m_s$.
- Extension: repeat with two springs in series and in parallel and check $k_\text{series} = (1/k_1 + 1/k_2)^{-1}$ and $k_\text{parallel} = k_1 + k_2$; or hang the phone on a long rubber band and show that its static line is curved (preview of 14.2).
