# Instructor notes — 14.6 Project: RLC resonance curve and Q factor

- Objective: the learner predicts, builds, measures and fits a series RLC resonance on battery power, derives R_total, L and C from the fit, and writes the module practical report linking it to the spring and to spectral line shapes.
- Likely confusion: omega0 versus f0 (the 2 pi); why the coil resistance belongs in R_total; why the ratio VR/Vin cancels source and meter effects only if both are read on the same range; the asymmetric half-power points of a low-Q resonance.
- Misconception: "Resonance means the voltage across the circuit is largest." With a real source, Vin is smallest at resonance, because the circuit impedance is lowest there; the current is largest.
- Analogy: pushing a child on a swing. Push at the swing's own rhythm and a small push builds a big swing (high current); push faster or slower and your pushes fight the motion. Friction in the chains (R) sets how big it can get and how fussy the timing is (Q).
- Expected hands-on result: example data: f0 = 1592.3 +/- 2.4 Hz, Q = 2.400 +/- 0.018, A = 0.2348, chi^2 6.6 for 17 dof, half-power 1295 and 1958 Hz, R_total 43.0 ohm, L 10.32 mH, C 0.968 uF (true 43.1 ohm, 10.3 mH, 0.970 uF in make_data.py). Predicted from printed values: 1592 Hz, Q 2.6. Real home builds: f0 within 10 % of 1592 Hz, Q 1.5 to 3; with 47 ohm, Q about 1.3. Safety check before sign-off: phone on battery, no earphones, non-polarised capacitor, no mains-powered device anywhere in the set-up.
- Extension: use C = 0.10 uF to raise Q (sqrt(L/C) = 316 ohm, f0 = 5.0 kHz) and test whether the meter still reads steadily; or record the ringdown of the LC circuit with a sound-card oscilloscope app on a battery-powered laptop and get Q from the decay, as in 14.2.
