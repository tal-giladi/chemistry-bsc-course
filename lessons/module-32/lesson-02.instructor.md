# Instructor notes — 32.2 Batteries, fuel cells and corrosion

- Objective: the learner calculates E, Q (mAh/g) and specific energy from thermodynamics and Faraday's law, explains voltage losses with 32.1's terms, and measures a corrosion current by Tafel extrapolation and Stern–Geary, converting it to mm/y.
- Likely confusion: mixed potential versus equilibrium potential; why icorr can't be measured directly with an ammeter (both currents flow on the same metal); the 3.6 factor in mAh/g; reciprocal sum for a cell's capacity.
- Misconception: "a dead battery is safe to open"; "aluminium always protects steel" (its oxide film often stops it acting as an anode); "stainless steel never corrodes" (pitting in chloride).
- Analogy: corrosion is a shop with its own cashier and customer on the same floor: money (electrons) moves inside the building, and nobody outside sees any current, but the stock (iron) still disappears.
- Expected hands-on result (generated data; true bare icorr 250 µA/cm², βa 70, βc 120, Ecorr −470 mV; inhibited 22 µA/cm², 85/130, −455 mV; Ru 1.0 Ω): bare 250 µA/cm² Tafel, 253 Stern–Geary (Rp 76 Ω cm²), 2.90 mm/y; uncorrected βa 106, icorr 408 (4.73 mm/y). Inhibited (used in 32.5): 21–22 µA/cm², Rp 1018, 0.25 mm/y. Home: copper-wrapped nail rusts most within 2–3 days; galvanised nail almost clean; aluminium patchy. Pencil: 57 %; 0.45 mm/y; 0.12 mm/y, 3.6 mm in 30 years, not enough.
- Safety: no batteries in any home part; WARNING box first in Hands-on; salt water, nails and copper wire only.
- Extension: put a few drops of red cabbage indicator around the copper-wrapped nail in salted agar or gelatine to see the alkaline (cathodic) zone turn green near the copper, a kitchen version of the ferroxyl test.
