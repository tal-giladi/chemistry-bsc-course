# Instructor notes — 16.3 Carboxylic acids and their derivatives

- Objective: carboxylic acid acidity and substituent effects; Henderson–Hasselbalch for drugs; addition–elimination mechanism; the reactivity ladder; Fischer esterification equilibrium; reactions of esters, amides and nitriles.
- Likely confusion: why an ester substitutes but a ketone adds. Point at the group on the carbonyl carbon: is it a leaving group (Cl, OR, OCOR, NR₂) or not (H, R)?
- Misconception: "the tetrahedral intermediate is a transition state". It is a real intermediate (it can be detected, and ¹⁸O exchange experiments show it reverts). Draw it with full bonds, not dotted lines.
- Analogy: the ladder as a waterfall: water flows downhill on its own (acid chloride → ester → amide), climbing needs a pump (SOCl₂ via the acid).
- Expected hands-on result: aspirin 1.01 % / 76.39 % / 99.99 % ionised at pH 1.5 / 4.0 / 7.4; benzoic acid 0.20 / 39.23 / 99.94 %. Fischer yields 66.7, 84.5, 90.3, 97.4 % for 1, 2, 3, 10 mol ethanol; 33.3 % with 5 mol water at the start. Drill 5/5.
- Data note: K ≈ 4 is the classic Berthelot-style teaching value; student measurements (e.g. the Truman State experiment) often give 2–4. Say so if a learner finds a different number.
- Extension: ask why esters of tertiary alcohols (tert-butyl esters) are cleaved by acid through a carbocation (SN1 at the alkyl carbon, not acyl substitution) and why that makes tert-butyl a useful protecting group for acids.
