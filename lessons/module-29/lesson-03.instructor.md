# Instructor notes — 29.3 Inhibition and regulation

- Objective: the learner writes the general reversible inhibition rate law, predicts apparent $K_m$ and $V_\text{max}$ for competitive, uncompetitive and mixed inhibitors, reads Lineweaver–Burk patterns, chooses the type by global fits and AICc, corrects IC50 with Cheng–Prusoff, and names the main modes of cellular regulation.
- Likely confusion: $K_i$ (binding to E) versus $K_i'$ (binding to ES); "noncompetitive" used for every mixed inhibitor in some texts; why an uncompetitive inhibitor lowers $K_m$ (it pulls ES away, so more substrate binds); IC50 versus $K_i$.
- Misconception: "the model with the lowest RSS wins" (the mixed model always has the lowest). Second: "a competitive drug is always the best choice" (quiz q4). Third: "an allosteric inhibitor is competitive because it lowers activity at low [S]".
- Analogy: a competitive inhibitor is someone sitting in your seat before you arrive — more ticket holders crowd them out; an uncompetitive inhibitor locks the door once you are seated — the more people sit down, the more get locked in.
- Expected hands-on result: Part A apparent $K_m$ 16.2, 30.8, 43.8, 75.1 mM at 0/50/100/200 mM fructose with $V_\text{max}$ 3.76–3.96; global competitive $V$ 3.86 ± 0.04, $K_m$ 16.2 ± 0.5, $K_i$ 56.8 ± 1.8 mM, AICc −177.5; mixed −174.6 ($K_i'$ → ∞); uncompetitive −61.1; IC50 130 mM at 20.8 mM sucrose, Cheng–Prusoff 57 mM; 640 mM at 166.7 mM. Part B $K_i$ = 5 µM (competitive), $K_i'$ = 2.0 mM (uncompetitive), noncompetitive, irreversible.
