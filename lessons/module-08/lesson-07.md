# 08.7 · Lab: coffee-cup calorimetry and a first titration

This is the module's practical. You will do two classic first-year experiments at your kitchen table: measure the heat absorbed when baking soda dissolves, using a foam-cup calorimeter you calibrate yourself, and find the acid content of vinegar by titrating it with baking-soda solution and red-cabbage indicator. Both use what you learned in this module: Hess's law gives the prediction, equilibrium and pH explain the indicator, and the redox lesson tells you what kind of reaction a titration is not. Both end in a number with an honest uncertainty, written up as a short lab report.

## Why it matters

A first chemistry lab is where a temperature reading or a colour change becomes a number with an error bar. Professional chemistry is full of this: a quality-control lab titrates every batch of vinegar, and an engineer measures a heat of reaction before scaling up a reactor. The skills are the same at home: calibrate, control the systematic errors you can, estimate the rest, repeat. What a kitchen cannot give you is the feel of a real burette and an analytical balance.

## Core ideas

> [!NOTE]
> The methods are stable. The baking-soda value $\Delta_\text{sol}H^\circ = +19.6$ kJ/mol comes from lesson 08.1 (OpenStax Appendix G and the NBS value for NaHCO₃(s)); safety data from PubChem; all checked 2026-10-17. The example data in the lab folder are generated by `make_data.py` from a stated model, not measured.

### The calorimeter has a heat capacity too

When salt dissolves in your cup, the heat comes from the water *and* from the cup, the lid and the thermometer. Together these behave like an extra mass of water. Their **heat capacity** $C_\text{cal}$ (J/K) is found by **calibration**: mix known masses of hot and cold water and see how far short of the ideal the final temperature falls.

$$m_\text{hot}\,c\,(T_\text{hot} - T_\text{f}) = (m_\text{cold}\,c + C_\text{cal})(T_\text{f} - T_\text{cold})$$

Heat lost by the hot water equals heat gained by the cold water and the cup (lesson [08.1](lesson-01.md)). Every value except $C_\text{cal}$ is measured, so you solve for it. For two nested foam cups, expect 20–60 J/K, about a tenth of the 418 J/K of 100 g of water.

### Extrapolate, do not just read the extremes

A foam cup still leaks heat slowly, and mixing takes time. So the lowest temperature you read is not the true final temperature. Instead, record the temperature every 30 s for a few minutes before mixing and several minutes after. Fit a straight line to each part and extrapolate both to the moment of mixing. The gap between the two lines at that moment is $\Delta T$. This corrects for the heat that leaked during mixing, and the scatter of the points about the lines gives the uncertainty. You did the same kind of fit in lesson [05.3](../module-05/lesson-03.md).

### Titration: counting moles with a colour change

A **titration** adds a solution of known concentration (the **titrant**) to a sample until the reaction between them is just complete: the **equivalence point**. An indicator changes colour at the **end point**, which should be as close to equivalence as possible.

$$\mathrm{CH_3COOH(aq) + NaHCO_3(aq) \rightarrow CH_3COONa(aq) + H_2O(l) + CO_2(g)}$$

It is 1 : 1, so moles of baking soda delivered equal moles of acetic acid in the sample. No oxidation number changes here (check: C, H, O and Na keep theirs), so it is an acid–base reaction, not redox like the vitamin C titration of lesson [08.5](lesson-05.md).

Halfway, the cup holds acetic acid and acetate in equal amounts: a buffer at pH 4.7 (lesson [08.4](lesson-04.md)), so the cabbage stays pink for a long time. At equivalence, the solution is sodium acetate with some dissolved CO₂, slightly basic. One drop of extra bicarbonate pushes it to about pH 8, where cabbage turns blue-green. Dissolved CO₂ is a weak acid and delays the change, so swirl well to drive it out (Le Chatelier, lesson [08.3](lesson-03.md)).

### Weigh, do not read volumes

A kitchen has no burette, but it has a scale. Weigh the vinegar sample, and weigh the syringe of titrant before and after: the difference is the titrant mass. Make the titrant by mass too (5.00 g of baking soda made up to 100.00 g of solution), so its concentration is in mol per gram. A 0.01 g scale on 7 g of titrant is 0.15 %, far better than reading a syringe scale. Lesson [01.5](../module-01/lesson-05.md) has the resolution and uncertainty of your scale.

### Two kinds of error

Repeating the titration three times shows the **random error** as the standard deviation. It does not show **systematic error**, such as an end point that always needs a little excess titrant or a cup that always loses heat while you pour. Those you must think about, and either correct (as calibration does for the cup) or estimate. Module 9 treats both in full.

## Worked example

### Calibration and dissolution, from the example data

The example file `calorimetry_example.csv` gives, extrapolated to the mixing time: calibration $T_\text{cold}$ = 21.00 °C, $T_\text{f}$ = 28.45 °C, with $m_\text{cold}$ = 100.0 g, $m_\text{hot}$ = 50.0 g and $T_\text{hot}$ = 45.0 °C.

$$C_\text{cal} = \frac{50.0 \times 4.184 \times (45.0 - 28.45)}{28.45 - 21.00} - 100.0 \times 4.184 = 464.7 - 418.4 = 46\ \text{J/K}$$

Dissolution of 5.00 g NaHCO₃ (0.0595 mol) in 100.0 g of water: before 22.47 °C, after 20.26 °C, so $\Delta T = -2.21$ K. The heat absorbed by the dissolving salt is taken from the water, the cup and the salt itself (about 1 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹):

$$q = (100.0 \times 4.184 + 46 + 5.00 \times 1.0) \times 2.21 = 1038\ \text{J}$$

$$\Delta_\text{sol}H = \frac{+1038\ \text{J}}{0.0595\ \text{mol}} = +17.4\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

Positive: endothermic, as predicted in lesson 08.1. The script propagates the uncertainties and gives ±0.7 kJ/mol. Ignoring the cup would give 15.7 kJ/mol, 10 % low. The table value, +19.6 kJ/mol, is for infinite dilution; at about 0.6 mol/kg the true heat of solution is a little lower, so a result of 16–19 kJ/mol is a good measurement.

A hidden systematic error: the model behind this data lets the hot water lose 0.5 K while being poured. That makes the fitted $C_\text{cal}$ 46 J/K instead of the true 30 J/K, yet it shifts $\Delta_\text{sol}H$ by only about 0.6 kJ/mol (3 %), because the cup is a small part of the total heat capacity. Knowing which errors matter is half the skill.

## Hands-on

What you need: two foam cups that nest, with a cardboard lid that has a hole for the thermometer probe; a digital kitchen thermometer (0.1 °C display); a kitchen scale (0.01 g pocket scale ideally, 0.1 g at least); baking soda; white vinegar (label "5 % acidity"); the red-cabbage indicator from lesson 08.4 (or a fresh batch); a 10 mL oral syringe; cups; a kettle; a timer; and the [Module 8 lab folder](../../labs/module-08/). Time: about 2.5 hours, including analysis and the report.

> [!WARNING]
> Mix hot water to about 45 °C before using it: hand-hot, not boiling. Wear safety glasses, especially while swirling vinegar and baking soda, which fizz and can spurt. Baking soda and household vinegar are food products (PubChem lists baking soda as not GHS-classified; concentrated acetic acid is corrosive, but 5 % vinegar is not). Never mix vinegar with bleach. Work on a tray and rinse everything away down the sink.

### Part 1 — simulations first (20 min)

[Simulation: Calorimetry — coffee-cup calorimeter](../../simulations/calorimetry/index.html?preset=coffee-cup)

Run a calibration, then dissolve a salt. Compare the extrapolated $\Delta T$ with the one you get from the lowest reading.

[Simulation: Titration bench — strong acid with a strong base](../../simulations/titration/index.html?preset=strong-acid)

Note the shape of the curve: flat, then a near-vertical jump at pH 7. Vinegar is a weak acid, so expect a buffered stretch at pH 4–5 before its jump.

### Part 2 — home: calibrate the cup and dissolve baking soda (50 min)

1. Weigh the empty nested cups. Add about 100 g of tap water and record the exact mass. Let it stand 5 minutes with the lid on.
2. Record the temperature every 30 s for 3 minutes (seven readings).
3. Meanwhile, mix kettle and tap water in a mug to about 45 °C. Weigh about 50 g of it into a second cup. Read its temperature, and at the 3-minute mark pour it all into the calorimeter at once. Note the exact time. Stir gently with the probe.
4. Keep reading every 30 s for 9 more minutes. Weigh the cup again to check the hot-water mass.
5. Empty and dry the cups. Repeat steps 1–2 with fresh 100 g of water. Weigh 5.00 g of baking soda on a piece of paper. At the 3-minute mark tip it in, note the time and stir steadily until it has all dissolved (about 1–2 minutes). Read every 30 s for 9 more minutes.
6. Type the readings into a CSV file with columns `run,t_s,T_C` and runs named `calibration` and `dissolution`, like `calorimetry_example.csv`.

How you know it worked: before mixing, the readings drift by under 0.1 °C per minute. The calibration jumps up by 7–8 K; the dissolution drops by about 2–2.5 K and then stays nearly flat.

### Part 3 — home: titrate vinegar (50 min)

1. **Titrant.** Weigh 5.00 g of baking soda into a clean cup and add water to a total of 100.00 g. Stir until fully dissolved. Concentration: $5.00 / 100.00 / 84.006 = 5.952 \times 10^{-4}$ mol/g.
2. **Sample.** Weigh about 5 g of vinegar into a cup and record the mass to 0.01 g. Add about 20 mL of water (it does not need to be exact) and 2 mL of cabbage indicator. It turns pink-red.
3. **Fill and weigh.** Fill the syringe with titrant, wipe it, and weigh it.
4. **Titrate.** Add titrant 1 mL at a time with swirling. Expect fizzing. After about 5 mL, add drops and swirl hard between them. The end point is the first blue-green that lasts 30 seconds. Expect about 7 g in total; refill and weigh again if needed.
5. **Weigh** the syringe again. The titrant mass is the difference.
6. Repeat steps 2–5 twice more (three trials). Record the data as `trial,m_vinegar_g,m_syringe_before_g,m_syringe_after_g`.

How you know it worked: each trial needs about 6.8–7.2 g of titrant per 5 g of vinegar, and the three results agree within about 2 %.

### Part 4 — analysis and report (30 min)

Open `lesson07_calorimetry.py` and `lesson07_titration.py` and complete the TODO functions (two in each). Edit the masses in each script's setup block to your own, then run:

```bash
py lesson07_calorimetry.py my_calorimetry.csv
py lesson07_titration.py my_titration.csv
```

With the example data they print $C_\text{cal}$ = 46 ± 9 J/K, $\Delta_\text{sol}H$ = 17.4 ± 0.7 kJ/mol, and vinegar at 4.96, 5.00 and 5.07 % (mean 5.01 %, SD 0.05 %, 95 % confidence interval ± 0.14 %). Write a short report with the [lab report template](../../templates/lab-report.md): aim, method in your own words, the data tables and plots, results with uncertainties, and a discussion of the main systematic errors.

<details><summary>Reference solutions and what a good discussion says</summary>

Calorimetry: `cup_heat_capacity` returns `m_hot * C_WATER * (T_hot - T_final) / (T_final - T_cold) - m_cold * C_WATER`; `dissolution_enthalpy` computes `q = (m_water * C_WATER + C_cup + m_salt * C_SALT) * (T_before - T_after)` and returns `q / (m_salt / M_NAHCO3) / 1000`. Titration: `acid_percent` returns `100 * m_titrant * w_titrant * M_ACETIC / m_vinegar`; `mean_sd` uses $n - 1$ in the standard deviation. Full code in the two `*_solution.py` files.

A good discussion names, for the calorimetry: heat lost while pouring (biases $C_\text{cal}$ high, small effect on $\Delta H$), slow dissolving (corrected by extrapolation), and the infinite-dilution table value. For the titration: the end point needs a slight excess of bicarbonate, so results come out about 1 % high (the example data were made with 4.95 % acid and give 5.01 %); dissolved CO₂ delays the colour change; the label's "5 % acidity" is per 100 mL, your result per 100 g (density about 1.005 g/mL). ## Common mistakes

- **Reading the coldest temperature as $T_\text{f}$.** Extrapolate both lines to the mixing time.
- **Forgetting the cup.** Leaving out $C_\text{cal}$ makes $\Delta_\text{sol}H$ about 10 % low.
- **Measuring $T_\text{hot}$ too early.** It cools while you wait; read it just before pouring.
- **Overshooting the end point.** Near the end, add one drop, swirl, wait. A whole millilitre past the end point is about a 15 % error.
- **Not swirling out CO₂.** The colour flips back to purple as CO₂ escapes; that is a false end point.
- **Reporting the SD as the full uncertainty.** It covers random scatter only; discuss systematic errors separately.

## Key takeaways

- A calorimeter must be calibrated; for foam cups its heat capacity is about a tenth of the water's.
- Extrapolating before and after lines to the mixing time corrects for slow mixing and heat leaks.
- Baking soda dissolving is endothermic, about +17 kJ/mol at this concentration, close to the Hess's-law prediction of +19.6.
- A mass-based titration with a kitchen scale reaches about 1 % precision; cabbage turns blue-green just past equivalence.
- Report a mean with SD and confidence interval, and discuss the systematic errors the repeats cannot reveal.
