# Instructor notes — 08.6 Electrochemical cells and the Nernst equation

- Objective: anode/cathode and cell notation; E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode; ΔG° = −nFE°, E° = (RT/nF) ln K; Nernst; build and characterise a lemon/vinegar/salt-water cell (E and internal resistance).
- Likely confusion: multiplying E° by coefficients, and double sign reversal of the anode potential. Make students write both half-reactions as reductions with table values, then subtract.
- Misconception: "the lemon cell is a Zn/Cu cell" (Goodisman 2001). Also "a battery runs down because it runs out of voltage": the voltage stays nearly flat until Q approaches K.
- Analogy: E° as the height of a waterfall (energy per litre), n F as the amount of water; ΔG is the total power available. Internal resistance as a narrow pipe at the top.
- Expected hands-on result: lemon and vinegar 0.8–1.0 V open circuit, salt water 0.7–0.85 V; under a 100 Ω load under 0.1 V; internal resistance 0.5–5 kΩ depending on electrode area and spacing. Script: E° 1.099 V, ΔG° −212 kJ/mol, K 1.40e37; 1.128, 1.040, 0.921 V; concentration cell 0.0887 V; lemon estimate 0.88–0.99 V; example fits lemon 0.919 V / 1761 Ω, vinegar 0.900 V / 2542 Ω, salt water 0.776 V / 884 Ω. Part C: 2.709 V; K = 3.6e15; 0.35 V.
- Safety: low voltage and kitchen materials only; nothing connected to mains; dispose of the zinc-containing lemon.
- Extension: corrosion as an unintended galvanic cell (a galvanised nail protects iron by being the anode); forward to Module 32 (Butler–Volmer, why real electrodes need overpotential) and Module 19 (voltammetry).
