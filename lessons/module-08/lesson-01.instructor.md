# Instructor notes — 08.1 Thermochemistry and Hess's law

- Objective: q = mcΔT with the system's sign convention; ΔrH° from ΔfH° (products minus reactants, with coefficients); Hess cycles; fuels per mole and per gram; predict the ΔT for baking soda dissolving (the 08.7 practical).
- Likely confusion: whose q. Students compute q for the water and report it as the reaction's ΔH with the same sign. Make them write "q_water = … so q_rxn = …" every time.
- Misconception: "breaking bonds releases energy" (from "ATP releases energy" or "burning releases energy"). Breaking always costs; making always releases; the net decides.
- Analogy: enthalpy as altitude. Hess's law is "the height difference between two towns does not depend on the road".
- Expected hands-on result: Part A 20.9 kJ to the room; propane −2220.0 kJ/mol, 50.3 kJ/g; N2O4 → 2NO2 +55.3 kJ; NH4NO3 +28.1 kJ/mol, 1.75 kJ per 5 g vs 1.17 kJ for NaHCO3. Script: CH4 −890.6, C3H8 −2220.0, C2H5OH −1366.9, H2 −285.8 kJ/mol; 55.5/50.3/29.7/141.8 kJ/g; Hess −393.51 both ways; NaHCO3 +19.6 kJ/mol, predicted ΔT −2.65 K for 5.00 g in 100 g water. Methanol extension −726.0 kJ/mol, 22.7 kJ/g.
- Data note: NaHCO3(s) is not in OpenStax Appendix G; −950.8 kJ/mol is the NBS value via Wikipedia. Measured integral heats of solution at finite concentration are somewhat lower than +19.6; expect home results of +15 to +20 kJ/mol.
- Extension: compute heat per litre for liquid fuels using densities (ethanol 0.789 g/mL) and compare with per-gram ranking; or the lattice-energy view of why some salts dissolve endothermically (forward to Module 18 Born–Haber).
