# Instructor notes — 25.2 Fluorescence: quinine in tonic water

- Objective: the learner sets up a quinine fluorescence method (350/450 nm, 90°, dilute H₂SO₄, ≥ 1 + 99 dilution), recognises inner filter and quenching in data, fits a Stern–Volmer constant, and chooses between external calibration and standard addition, judging a result against 83 mg/L.
- Likely confusion: that a "stronger" sample can give a weaker signal; the flask factor in standard addition (5 mL in 10 mL doubles the result); why sulfuric and not hydrochloric acid.
- Misconception: "standard addition fixes every matrix problem". It corrects multiplicative effects (quenching, absorbing matrix), not additive ones (a fluorescent impurity, a wrong blank), and it assumes a linear response.
- Analogy: the inner filter is a crowd at a concert: the people at the front take all the view, so adding more people at the back does not add more viewers of the stage.
- Expected hands-on result: example data — dilution series on the low-range line up to 1/16, 92 % at 1/8, 41 % at full tonic; K_SV ≈ 138 L/mol (true 130), intercept 0.88; external 72.3 ± 0.2 mg/L, standard addition 72.9 ± 0.6 mg/L (95 % ± 1.9), true 72.0; below 83 mg/L. Home: glow concentrated at the entry side of full tonic; K_SV 100–200 L/mol typical; 395 nm torches give a dimmer glow than 365 nm.
- Safety emphasis: insist on the "never look into it or at reflections" rule; check the torch on paper, not by looking at it. No tasting.
- Extension: diet versus regular tonic (sugar does not quench); try a vitamin B2 tablet dissolved in water (riboflavin glows yellow-green) as a second fluorophore and show the emission colour differs; compute the expected inner-filter curve with a = 1.0 and overlay it.
