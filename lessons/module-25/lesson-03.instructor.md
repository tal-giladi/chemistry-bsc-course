# Instructor notes — 25.3 Atomic absorption and emission

- Objective: the learner runs a flame AAS determination in order with reasons, diagnoses chemical, ionisation, spectral and physical interferences and picks the fix, computes a result with uncertainty, a spike recovery and a check-standard drift, and distinguishes lines, bands and continua with the phone spectrometer.
- Likely confusion: releasing agent versus ionisation suppressor (both are "add an excess of another element"); why a low spike recovery need not mean a matrix effect; why the sodium D doublet looks like one line.
- Misconception: "a hotter flame is always better". It frees refractory compounds but ionises easily ionised elements, which then need a suppressor.
- Analogy: lanthanum is a decoy partner: phosphate prefers to hold on to lanthanum, so calcium is free to go into the flame alone.
- Expected hands-on result: residuals of an all-standard line are curved; 0–6 mg/L line A = −0.0009 + 0.03992 c, s_r 0.0020, LOD 0.16 mg/L; water 48.0 ± 0.4 mg/L (s), true 48.0; no-La 39.9 mg/L (17 % low); spike recovery 90 % (out of range), 97 % with quadratic; check drift −0.9 %. Home: Na line at 587–591 nm with a 25.1 calibration; no K lines; light salt still yellow.
- Safety emphasis: gas hob only, adult present, no methanol or alcohol flames, arm's length, glasses.
- Extension: compute the Boltzmann ratio for Na at 2300 K (Lesson 19.2) to explain why emission of Na is easy and of Zn is hard; or redo the spike at 1.0 mg/L in the simulation and show recovery near 100 %.
