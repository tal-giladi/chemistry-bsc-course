# 23.3 · Equilibrium constant by spectrophotometry

An equilibrium constant is a number you can measure, not only look up. If one species in an equilibrium is coloured and the others are not, a spectrophotometer reads its concentration directly through Beer–Lambert's law, and an ICE table gives you everything else. This lesson works through the classic physical chemistry experiment: the blood-red complex formed by iron(III) and thiocyanate. You will run it in the simulation, analyse the data in Python, correct a systematic error that many student reports miss, and check the stoichiometry with a Job plot.

## Why it matters

Many constants in the tables were measured exactly this way: acid dissociation constants of indicators and drugs, binding constants of metal complexes, protein–ligand affinities. A pharmacologist who reports that a drug binds its target with a certain affinity is reporting an equilibrium constant, and often a spectroscopic one. The decisions are the same as here: which wavelength, which concentrations, how to calibrate, and how to tell from your own numbers whether the model behind them is right. A constant that drifts across your series is telling you something.

## Core ideas

> [!NOTE]
> The method is stable. The wavelength (447 nm) is the one used in standard teaching labs. The formation constant of FeSCN²⁺ depends on the acid concentration and ionic strength (Analyst, 1982), so no single literature value fits every recipe; the simulation and the example data use K = 140 and ε = 4600 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹ as illustrative values of the size teaching labs report. Checked 2026-10-17. The example data are generated by `make_data.py`, not measured.

### The reaction and the constant

$$\mathrm{Fe^{3+}(aq) + SCN^-(aq) \rightleftharpoons FeSCN^{2+}(aq)} \qquad K = \frac{[\mathrm{FeSCN^{2+}}]}{[\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}][\mathrm{SCN^-}]}$$

Fe³⁺ in acid is pale yellow, thiocyanate is colourless, and the complex absorbs strongly near 447 nm. At that wavelength only the complex absorbs, so its absorbance $A$ in a 1 cm cell gives its concentration: $x = [\mathrm{FeSCN^{2+}}] = A/(\varepsilon b)$ (lesson [19.1](../module-19/lesson-01.md)).

### Two kinds of solution

**Standards** find $\varepsilon b$. Put a large excess of Fe³⁺ (0.2 M) with small amounts of SCN⁻ (up to 2 × 10⁻⁴ M). By Le Chatelier (lesson [08.3](../module-08/lesson-03.md)), almost all the SCN⁻ is pushed into the complex, so $x \approx [\mathrm{SCN^-}]_0$. The slope of $A$ against $[\mathrm{SCN^-}]_0$ is $\varepsilon b$.

**Mixtures** find $K$. Here Fe³⁺ and SCN⁻ are at comparable concentrations (10⁻³ M and 2–10 × 10⁻⁴ M), so a real fraction stays unreacted. Each absorbance gives $x$, and an **ICE table** (initial, change, equilibrium) gives the rest:

| | Fe³⁺ | SCN⁻ | FeSCN²⁺ |
|---|---|---|---|
| initial | $[\mathrm{Fe}]_0$ | $[\mathrm{SCN}]_0$ | 0 |
| change | $-x$ | $-x$ | $+x$ |
| equilibrium | $[\mathrm{Fe}]_0 - x$ | $[\mathrm{SCN}]_0 - x$ | $x$ |

The "initial" concentrations are those *after mixing*: dilute every stock by its share of the total volume first.

### Why the acid, and why it must be constant

The Fe³⁺ stock is made in nitric acid (about 0.5 M) for two reasons. Without acid, Fe³⁺ hydrolyses to brown FeOH²⁺, a second absorbing species that ruins the model. And the acid sets a nearly constant **ionic strength**. A K written with concentrations is really a ratio of activities times a ratio of activity coefficients (lesson [13.6](../module-13/lesson-06.md)). The coefficients depend on ionic strength, so K only stays constant across the series if ionic strength does. That is also why published values differ: each was measured in a different medium.

### The systematic error in the standards

"Almost all" is not "all". With $[\mathrm{Fe}]_0$ = 0.200 M and K = 140, the fraction of thiocyanate complexed is

$$\frac{K[\mathrm{Fe}]_0}{1 + K[\mathrm{Fe}]_0} = \frac{28}{29} = 0.966$$

So the standards read 3.4 % low, the fitted $\varepsilon b$ is 3.4 % low, and every $x$ from the mixtures is 3.4 % high. Because $x$ appears on top and (subtracted) at the bottom of K, K comes out 4–5 % high. The fix is a short loop: compute K, use it to correct $\varepsilon b$, recompute K, and repeat until it stops changing. Two or three rounds are enough.

### Is the model right? Spread, trend and a Job plot

If the 1:1 model is right, every mixture gives the same K within its noise. A K that rises or falls steadily across the series is a warning: a second complex (Fe(SCN)₂⁺ forms at high thiocyanate), hydrolysis, or a wrong dilution. A **Job plot** (method of continuous variations) tests the stoichiometry directly. Keep $[\mathrm{Fe}]_0 + [\mathrm{SCN}]_0$ fixed and vary the mole fraction of iron from 0.1 to 0.9. The absorbance peaks at the mole fraction that matches the formula: 0.5 for a 1:1 complex, 0.33 for FeL₂.

### What can go wrong

- **Wrong blank.** Blank with the same acid and Fe³⁺ concentration as the samples; Fe³⁺ itself absorbs a little at 447 nm.
- **Readings outside the good range.** Absorbances below 0.1 have a large relative error; keep the mixtures between about 0.1 and 1 (lesson 19.1).
- **Colour that fades.** The complex is slowly reduced in light and over time. Read within about 15 minutes of mixing, in the same order every time.

Safety reasoning for the real lab: the iron stock is in nitric acid, corrosive to skin and eyes, so gloves and goggles. Potassium thiocyanate is harmful if swallowed, inhaled or on skin (PubChem: H302, H312, H332) and very toxic to aquatic life (H400), so all waste goes to a collection bottle, not the sink. There is no home version: the reagents are lab chemicals, not kitchen ones.

## Worked example

The example standards give $A = 0.0016 + 4432\,[\mathrm{SCN^-}]_0$. Mixture 3: $[\mathrm{Fe}]_0 = 1.00 \times 10^{-3}$ M, $[\mathrm{SCN}]_0 = 6.00 \times 10^{-4}$ M after mixing, $A = 0.321$.

**Naive K.** $x = (0.321 - 0.0016)/4432 = 7.21 \times 10^{-5}$ M. Then $[\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}] = 9.28 \times 10^{-4}$ M, $[\mathrm{SCN^-}] = 5.28 \times 10^{-4}$ M, and

$$K = \frac{7.21\times10^{-5}}{(9.28\times10^{-4})(5.28\times10^{-4})} = 147$$

**Corrected K.** With K ≈ 147 the standards were 96.7 % complexed, so $\varepsilon b = 4432/0.967 = 4583$. After two more rounds it settles at $\varepsilon b$ = 4590 L/mol and K = 141 for this mixture. Over all five mixtures: K = 140, SD 3, 95 % interval ± 3, with no trend. The correction (5 %) is larger than the whole random uncertainty (2 %): it is the error that matters.

**Sensitivity.** Only 7 % of the iron is complexed, so $[\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}]$ barely depends on $x$, but $[\mathrm{SCN^-}]$ does. An absorbance error of 0.003 changes $x$ by 1 % and K by about 1.2 %. Mixtures where most of the SCN⁻ is complexed would make K very sensitive to A; that is why the mixtures use similar concentrations of both ions.

### Part A — simulation: the iron thiocyanate bench (30 min)

[Simulation: Spectrometer — FeSCN²⁺ equilibrium constant](../../simulations/spectrometer/index.html?preset=equilibrium)

1. **Choose the wavelength.** Scan a mixture from 380 to 600 nm and confirm the maximum near 447 nm. Set the instrument there.
2. **Blank.** Use 0.5 M HNO₃ containing the same Fe³⁺ as the standards. Then blank the mixtures with 0.5 M HNO₃ containing 1 × 10⁻³ M Fe³⁺.
3. **Standards.** Make six standards in 0.200 M Fe³⁺ with $[\mathrm{SCN}]_0$ from 0 to 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ M. Read each.
4. **Mixtures.** Make five mixtures with 1.00 × 10⁻³ M Fe³⁺ and $[\mathrm{SCN}]_0$ = 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 × 10⁻⁴ M (all after mixing). Read each within 15 minutes.
5. **Job series.** Keep the total at 2.0 × 10⁻³ M and vary the iron mole fraction from 0.1 to 0.9.
6. **Break it on purpose.** Make one mixture without nitric acid and one with 20 × 10⁻⁴ M SCN⁻. Note what happens to K.
7. Export as CSV with columns `type,Fe0_M,SCN0_M,A447` (types `standard`, `mix`, `job`) and save as `my_fescn.csv`.

How you know it worked: the standards lie on a straight line through (almost) zero, the mixtures read between about 0.1 and 0.5, and the Job plot peaks at 0.5. The no-acid mixture gives a K far from the others.

### Part C — pencil (20 min)

1. You mix 5.00 mL of 2.00 × 10⁻³ M Fe(NO₃)₃ in HNO₃ with 3.00 mL of 2.00 × 10⁻³ M KSCN and 2.00 mL of HNO₃. The absorbance is 0.276 with $\varepsilon b$ = 4590 L/mol. Find K.
2. A student uses standards with only 0.0200 M Fe³⁺. By roughly how much is their K wrong if they skip the correction, and in which direction?
3. K across a student's series rises from 120 to 190 as $[\mathrm{SCN}]_0$ increases. Name one chemical cause.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. After mixing (10.00 mL total): $[\mathrm{Fe}]_0 = 1.00 \times 10^{-3}$ M, $[\mathrm{SCN}]_0 = 6.00 \times 10^{-4}$ M. $x = 0.276/4590 = 6.01 \times 10^{-5}$ M. $K = 6.01\times10^{-5} / (9.40\times10^{-4} \times 5.40\times10^{-4}) = 118$.
2. $K[\mathrm{Fe}]_0 = 2.8$, so only $2.8/3.8 = 74$ % is complexed. Their $\varepsilon b$ is 26 % low, every $x$ is 35 % high, and K comes out roughly 45 % high (around 200 instead of 140). This is why the excess must be large, or corrected for.
3. At high thiocyanate a second complex, Fe(SCN)₂⁺, forms and also absorbs. The 1:1 model then credits all the colour to FeSCN²⁺, and K appears to grow with $[\mathrm{SCN}]_0$. A changing ionic strength across the series would also make K drift.

</details>

## Common mistakes

- **Using stock concentrations in the ICE table.** Dilute every stock to the total volume first; otherwise K is wrong by the dilution factor squared.
- **Assuming the standards are 100 % complexed.** Check $K[\mathrm{Fe}]_0$; if it is under about 100, correct $\varepsilon b$ and iterate.
- **Blanking with water.** Fe³⁺ absorbs slightly at 447 nm; blank with the same iron and acid as the samples.
- **Reporting K without the medium.** A K for FeSCN²⁺ means little without the acid concentration, ionic strength and temperature.
- **Averaging a trend away.** If K drifts steadily across the series, the mean is not the answer; find the cause.

## Key takeaways

- If only one species absorbs, Beer–Lambert gives its equilibrium concentration and an ICE table gives the rest.
- Standards need a large excess of one reagent; mixtures need comparable concentrations so that both reagents remain in measurable amounts.
- Incomplete complexation in the standards biases K by several per cent, often more than the random error; correct it by iteration.
- A constant K across the series and a Job maximum at 0.5 are the evidence that the 1:1 model holds.
- Concentration-based K values depend on ionic strength, so they are only comparable in the same medium.
