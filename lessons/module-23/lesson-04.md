# 23.4 · Kinetics: dye fading tracked with a phone camera

Household bleach destroys food dyes: drop blue colouring into dilute bleach and the colour slowly disappears. That fading is a clean kinetics experiment. The bleach is in large excess, so the dye decays with a single exponential, and its colour can be read from phone photos with the method you built in lesson 09.6. In this lesson you measure the rate constant, show that the rate is first order in hypochlorite by doubling the bleach, and estimate an activation energy from a warmer run. It is a real experiment, done at home with a phone, but bleach demands respect: the safety rules come first.

## Why it matters

Kinetics is measured, not derived. Every rate law in Module 22 started as a series of runs like these: change one concentration, keep the rest fixed, watch how the rate responds. The same isolation method is used to study how drugs degrade in solution, how fast pollutants are destroyed in water treatment, and how quickly a dye in a textile fades. Water utilities that disinfect with chlorine need exactly these rate constants to know how long the water must stay in contact. And a phone-based colour measurement, done carefully, gives rate constants good to a few per cent.

## Core ideas

> [!NOTE]
> The kinetics and the colour method are stable. Household bleach strengths differ by brand and fall as the bottle ages, so your rate constants will differ from the example; the example data are generated by `make_data.py`, not measured. Hazard data are from PubChem (checked 2026-10-17). Phone cameras change how they process images with software updates; lock exposure and white balance and treat the camera as a relative instrument.

### Isolation: making a second-order reaction look first-order

The dye D (here Brilliant Blue FCF, E133, the usual blue food colour) reacts with hypochlorite. Assume the rate law

$$v = -\frac{d[\mathrm{D}]}{dt} = k[\mathrm{D}][\mathrm{OCl^-}]^n$$

A few drops of food colouring give about 10⁻⁵ M dye. Two millilitres of household bleach (about 4 % sodium hypochlorite, 0.5 M) in 200 mL of water gives about 5 × 10⁻³ M hypochlorite: hundreds of times more. While all the dye reacts, $[\mathrm{OCl^-}]$ falls by well under 1 %, so it is effectively constant. Then

$$-\frac{d[\mathrm{D}]}{dt} = k'[\mathrm{D}], \qquad k' = k[\mathrm{OCl^-}]^n, \qquad [\mathrm{D}] = [\mathrm{D}]_0\,e^{-k't}$$

This is **pseudo-first-order** behaviour (lesson [22.1](../module-22/lesson-01.md)): an exponential decay with half-life $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/k'$. Measuring $k'$ at two hypochlorite concentrations gives $n$:

$$n = \frac{\ln(k'_2/k'_1)}{\ln([\mathrm{OCl^-}]_2/[\mathrm{OCl^-}]_1)}$$

Doubling the bleach and seeing $k'$ double means $n = 1$.

### From photo to absorbance

The blue dye absorbs red light, so the red value of a photo falls as dye concentration rises. As in lesson [09.6](../module-09/lesson-06.md), undo the sRGB coding to get linear intensity $I$, and compare with a photo of the same cup of water with no dye (the blank):

$$A = -\log_{10}\frac{I}{I_\text{blank}}$$

This **pseudo-absorbance** is proportional to concentration over the range where Beer–Lambert holds (lesson [19.1](../module-19/lesson-01.md)). For first-order kinetics you do not even need a calibration: $A$ is proportional to $[\mathrm{D}]$, and the rate constant is the same whatever the proportionality factor.

### The offset and how to fit it

The colour never quite reaches the blank. A slightly coloured product, reflections and stray light leave a small residual absorbance $A_\infty$. Fit

$$A(t) = A_0\,e^{-k't} + A_\infty$$

directly with nonlinear least squares, or plot $\ln(A - A_\infty)$ against $t$ and read $-k'$ from the slope. Forgetting $A_\infty$ makes the ln-plot curve at late times and biases $k'$ low. The early part of the decay, the first two half-lives, carries most of the information.

### Temperature: a rough Arrhenius estimate

Rate constants rise with temperature. Two runs, at $T_1$ and $T_2$, give

$$E_a = \frac{R\ln(k'_2/k'_1)}{1/T_1 - 1/T_2}$$

Two points give no check of the straight line; lesson [22.4](../module-22/lesson-04.md) does it properly with many temperatures. An open cup also drifts towards room temperature, so read the temperature at the start and end and use the mean.

### Why each step, and what can go wrong

- **Same cup, same place, locked camera.** The blank and every photo must see the same light. Auto-exposure brightens a fading cup and hides the change.
- **Dilute the bleach first, then add the dye.** Start the clock when the dye goes in and is stirred, so the mixing time is a small fraction of the half-life.
- **Pure blue only.** Green colouring is blue plus yellow (tartrazine), and the two fade at different rates; the decay is then a sum of two exponentials.
- **Same bleach bottle for all runs.** Hypochlorite decomposes as bleach ages and its pH matters, so two bottles are two different reagents.
- **Light.** Strong sunlight speeds up hypochlorite decomposition. Work in diffuse daylight, out of direct sun.

## Worked example

Run A of the example data: 2.0 mL bleach in 200 mL water at 20 °C. The two blank photos average R = 231.05; at $t$ = 0 the dyed cup reads R = 122.1 and at 240 s R = 169.6.

**Pseudo-absorbance.** Undo sRGB: 231.05 → $I_\text{blank}$ = 0.799; 122.1 → 0.195; 169.6 → 0.400. So $A(0) = -\log_{10}(0.195/0.799) = 0.613$ and $A(240) = -\log_{10}(0.400/0.799) = 0.301$.

**A quick rate constant.** With $A_\infty$ = 0.012 from the fit, $\ln\frac{0.613 - 0.012}{0.301 - 0.012} = \ln 2.08 = 0.732$ over 240 s, so $k' \approx 3.05 \times 10^{-3}$ s⁻¹ and $t_{1/2} \approx 3.9$ min. The full fit over all 31 photos gives $k'$ = (2.99 ± 0.02) × 10⁻³ s⁻¹.

**Order in hypochlorite.** Run B, with 4.0 mL of bleach, gives $k'$ = 6.04 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹. $n = \ln(6.04/2.99)/\ln 2 = 1.01$: first order in hypochlorite, so the full rate law is $v = k[\mathrm{D}][\mathrm{OCl^-}]$, second order overall. With $[\mathrm{OCl^-}] \approx 5.4 \times 10^{-3}$ M in run A, $k \approx 2.99\times10^{-3}/5.4\times10^{-3} = 0.55$ M⁻¹ s⁻¹ for this (illustrative) bleach.

**Activation energy.** Run C, 2.0 mL at 30 °C, gives $k'$ = 5.89 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹.

$$E_a = \frac{8.314 \times \ln(5.89/2.99)}{1/293.15 - 1/303.15} = \frac{8.314 \times 0.678}{1.125 \times 10^{-4}} = 50\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

A 10 K warming nearly doubles the rate, which is the old rule of thumb for $E_a$ near 50 kJ/mol. The fit uncertainties look tiny (under 1 %), but repeated real runs scatter by 5–10 % because of temperature drift and lighting; quote the spread of repeats, not the fit error.

## Hands-on

What you need: plain unscented household bleach (sodium hypochlorite, typically 3–6 %; read the label and do not use "thick" bleach with added detergent), blue food colouring (E133 only, not green), tap water, three identical clear plastic cups, a 5 mL or 10 mL oral syringe kept only for this experiment and labelled "bleach", a kitchen thermometer, a timer, white paper, a phone on a stand (as in 09.6), safety glasses, household rubber gloves, the [Module 23 lab folder](../../labs/module-23/), `rgb_from_photos.py` from the [Module 9 lab folder](../../labs/module-09/), and the [lab report template](../../templates/lab-report.md). Time: about 2 hours.

> [!WARNING]
> **Never mix bleach with acids or with ammonia.** Bleach with vinegar, citric acid, lemon juice, descalers or toilet cleaners releases chlorine gas; bleach with ammonia, glass cleaners or urine releases chloramine gases. Both damage the lungs. Use only bleach, water and food colouring in this experiment, nothing else, and put away the citric acid from lesson 23.2 before you start.
> Use small amounts: never more than 10 mL of bleach in total, always added to a lot of water (200 mL), never water added to bleach. Work by an open window or with a fan running, so the air keeps moving. Wear safety glasses and gloves: bleach burns eyes and skin (PubChem: H314, H318). Use plastic cups, not metal. Keep children and pets away and do not leave cups unattended. Afterwards pour everything down the sink with plenty of running water, rinse the cups and syringe, and wash your hands. If bleach gets in your eyes, rinse with water for 15 minutes and get medical help.

### Part A — simulation first (15 min)

[Simulation: Kinetics — food dye fading in dilute bleach](../../simulations/kinetics/index.html?preset=dye-fading)

Run three cases: 2 mL bleach at 20 °C, 4 mL at 20 °C, and 2 mL at 30 °C. Task: read $k'$ from each, confirm that doubling the bleach doubles $k'$, and estimate $E_a$. Then try 0.2 mL of bleach (only a few times more hypochlorite than dye) and watch the decay stop being a single exponential: isolation has failed.

### Part B — home: three runs (60 min)

1. **Set up the camera** as in lesson 09.6: phone fixed about 20 cm above the cup, white paper underneath, diffuse daylight, no flash, exposure and white balance locked.
2. **Blank.** Put 200 mL of tap water at about 20 °C in cup 1. Photograph it twice. These are the blank photos for run A.
3. **Add the bleach.** With gloves and glasses on, draw 2.0 mL of bleach into the syringe and squirt it into the water. Stir gently with a plastic spoon. Write down the temperature.
4. **Add the dye and start the clock.** Add 2 drops of blue colouring, stir for 5 seconds, and take the first photo at $t$ = 0. Photograph every 30 s for 15 minutes without moving the cup or the phone.
5. **Run B.** Repeat steps 2–4 in cup 2 with 4.0 mL of bleach.
6. **Run C.** Repeat with 2.0 mL of bleach in 200 mL of hand-warm tap water, about 30 °C and no hotter. Read the temperature at the start and the end and use the mean.
7. **Read the colours.** Copy `rgb_from_photos.py` into the Module 23 folder and run `py rgb_from_photos.py blank1.jpg blank2.jpg t000.jpg t030.jpg` (and so on). Enter the values in `my_dye_fading.csv` with columns `run,type,T_C,bleach_mL,t_s,R,G,B` (type `blank` or `sample`; see `dye_fading_example.csv`).

If the colour is gone in under 2 minutes, halve the bleach in every run; if it has barely changed after 10 minutes, double it. Keep the 1 : 2 ratio between runs A and B and never exceed the 10 mL total.

How you know it worked: the red value rises smoothly towards the blank value, green and blue change much less, and the cup ends nearly colourless.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing bleach with anything but water and dye.** Acids give chlorine, ammonia gives chloramines. This is the one mistake in this lesson that can hurt you.
- **Letting the phone choose the exposure.** The camera brightens the image as the dye fades and flattens the curve. Lock exposure, or put a white card in every frame and normalise to it.
- **Using raw sRGB values as intensity.** Without undoing the sRGB curve the "absorbance" is not proportional to concentration and the decay is not exponential.
- **Ignoring $A_\infty$.** The ln-plot bends at late times and $k'$ comes out low. Fit the offset or use only the first two half-lives.
- **Too little bleach.** With hypochlorite only a few times the dye, its concentration falls during the run and the kinetics are no longer pseudo-first-order.
- **Green or mixed colouring.** Two dyes, two rate constants, one curved ln-plot.

## Key takeaways

- With hypochlorite in large excess, the dye decays as a single exponential with rate constant $k' = k[\mathrm{OCl^-}]^n$.
- Doubling the bleach doubles $k'$: the reaction is first order in hypochlorite and second order overall.
- A phone photo, linearised from sRGB and compared with a blank, gives a pseudo-absorbance good enough for rate constants to a few per cent.
- Two temperatures give only a rough activation energy; a 10 K rise that doubles the rate means $E_a$ near 50 kJ/mol.
- Bleach stays safe when it is diluted, used in small amounts, kept away from acids and ammonia, and used in moving air.
