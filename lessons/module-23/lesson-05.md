# 23.5 · Electrochemistry: cells, potentials and a lemon battery

In lesson 08.6 you built a lemon cell, read its voltage and learned that copper only hosts the reduction of hydrogen ions. This lesson treats the cell as a physical chemist does: as a voltage source with an internal resistance, measured properly with a load line and a fit. You will compare lemon juice, vinegar and salt water as electrolytes, join cells in series and parallel, and then use laboratory data from a zinc–copper cell to recover the Nernst equation's slope, which is how potentiometry turns a voltage into a concentration.

## Why it matters

Every battery datasheet quotes two numbers that this lesson measures: an open-circuit voltage and an internal resistance. Together they decide how much current a battery can give, how much it heats, and why a phone shuts down on a cold day while the battery still shows charge. In the analytical lab the same physics sits behind every pH meter and ion-selective electrode: they measure a cell potential at almost zero current, and convert it to a concentration with the Nernst slope. A pH meter that reads 55 mV per pH unit instead of 59 is a meter that needs a new electrode.

## Core ideas

> [!NOTE]
> Cell theory and the Nernst equation are stable. Standard potentials are from OpenStax Appendix L as in lesson 08.6; $F$ and $R$ are CODATA 2022 exact values; checked 2026-10-17. Home cells vary with the fruit, the nails and the wire; the example data are generated by `make_data.py`, not measured.

### EMF and terminal voltage

A cell drives charge with an **electromotive force** (EMF) $E$: the cell potential of lesson [08.6](../module-08/lesson-06.md), set by the chemistry. Inside the cell the current must flow through the electrolyte and across the electrode surfaces, and that costs voltage. Model it as an **internal resistance** $r$ in series with an ideal source. With a load resistor $R$ connected, a current $I$ flows and the voltage across the terminals is

$$V = E - Ir = E\,\frac{R}{R + r}$$

At open circuit ($I = 0$) $V = E$. Under a heavy load ($R \ll r$) almost all the EMF is lost inside. A digital multimeter on its voltage range draws almost no current (its input resistance is about 10 MΩ), so it reads close to $E$. That is the principle of **potentiometry**: measure a cell's potential without drawing current, so the reading is the thermodynamic value and not the value minus losses.

### The load line

Connect several resistors, one at a time, and read $V$ across each. Ohm's law gives the current, $I = V/R$ (lesson [04.4](../module-04/lesson-04.md)). A plot of $V$ against $I$ is a straight line, the **load line**: intercept $E$, slope $-r$. A least-squares fit gives both with uncertainties (lesson [09.4](../module-09/lesson-04.md)). The fit is better than a single open-circuit reading, because it uses every point and shows whether the model holds.

The power delivered to the load is $P = I^2 R = E^2 R/(R + r)^2$. It is largest when $R = r$:

$$P_\text{max} = \frac{E^2}{4r}$$

For a lemon cell with $E$ = 0.94 V and $r$ = 1.8 kΩ that is about 120 µW: enough for a digital clock, nowhere near enough for a bulb.

### What sets r and E in a home cell

- **The electrolyte's conductivity.** Salt water conducts much better than lemon juice, so $r$ is lower. More salt lowers $r$ further, up to a point.
- **Electrode area and spacing.** Doubling the wetted area roughly halves $r$; moving the electrodes closer lowers it too.
- **The cathode reaction.** In lemon juice and vinegar, H⁺ is reduced at the copper (Goodisman, 2001). In neutral salt water there is little H⁺, so the copper reduces dissolved oxygen and water instead, and the EMF is lower, typically 0.7–0.8 V.
- **Polarisation.** Under load, hydrogen bubbles and depleted ions near the electrodes make the voltage sag with time. Read each point after the same short wait (about 10 s), and go back to open circuit between loads.

### Series and parallel

Cells in **series** (copper of one to zinc of the next) add their EMFs and their internal resistances: $N$ identical cells give $NE$ and $Nr$. Cells in **parallel** keep $E$ and divide $r$ by $N$. The maximum power $E^2/4r$ grows by $N$ either way; what changes is the voltage at which it comes. A red LED needs about 1.8 V before it conducts, so it needs at least two lemon cells in series, and three or four to glow visibly.

### The Nernst slope and potentiometry

For a cell $\mathrm{Zn\,|\,Zn^{2+}\,||\,Cu^{2+}\,|\,Cu}$ the Nernst equation (lesson 08.6) gives

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln\frac{[\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}]}{[\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}]} = \text{const} + \frac{2.303\,RT}{nF}\log_{10}[\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}]$$

with $[\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}]$ held constant. A plot of $E$ against $\log_{10}[\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}]$ is a straight line with slope $2.303RT/nF$: 59.16 mV per decade divided by $n$ at 25 °C. With $n$ = 2 for copper, 29.58 mV per decade. Every ion-selective electrode is calibrated the same way: measure standards, fit the slope, then read unknowns off the line. A slope well below the theoretical one means a tired electrode or interfering ions.

Two real-lab details. The salt bridge adds a small **liquid-junction potential**, a few millivolts. And the Nernst equation strictly uses activities, not concentrations (lesson [13.6](../module-13/lesson-06.md)); at 0.1 M a divalent ion's activity coefficient is well below 1. Both shift the intercept more than the slope, which is one reason analysts fit the slope rather than trust tables.

## Worked example

### 1. A lemon cell's load line

From the example data: with 10 kΩ the lemon cell reads 0.792 V; with 1 kΩ it reads 0.328 V.

$$I_{10k} = \frac{0.792}{10\,000} = 79.2\ \mu\text{A}, \qquad I_{1k} = \frac{0.328}{1000} = 328\ \mu\text{A}$$

$$r = -\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta I} = \frac{0.792 - 0.328}{(328 - 79.2) \times 10^{-6}} = 1865\ \Omega, \qquad E = 0.792 + 79.2\times10^{-6} \times 1865 = 0.940\ \text{V}$$

The fit over all seven resistors gives $E$ = 0.936 ± 0.018 V and $r$ = 1815 ± 52 Ω; the open-circuit reading is 0.933 V. Two points give a fair estimate; seven give it with an uncertainty and a check that the line is straight.

### 2. Three lemons for an LED

In series: $E$ = 3 × 0.936 = 2.81 V, $r$ = 3 × 1815 = 5.4 kΩ. A red LED holds about 1.8 V across itself, so the current is at most $(2.81 - 1.8)/5400 = 0.19$ mA: a faint glow in a dark room. Four in parallel instead would give 0.94 V, too little to light it at all, but a quarter of the resistance.

### 3. n from the Nernst slope

The example lab data, Zn | Zn²⁺ (0.100 M) || Cu²⁺ (c) | Cu, give $E = 1.1325 + 0.02964\log_{10}c$. Then

$$n = \frac{2.303 RT}{F \times \text{slope}} = \frac{0.05916}{0.02964} = 2.00$$

At $c$ = 0.100 M (equal concentrations) the line gives 1.103 V against the standard 1.099 V; the 4 mV is the kind of offset a junction potential produces.

1. **Build three cells.** Cup 1: a lemon cut in half, nail and wire pushed in 2 cm apart and 2 cm deep. Cup 2: 100 mL of water with 5 g of salt. Cup 3: 100 mL of white vinegar. In cups 2 and 3, hang the nail and wire over the rim 2 cm apart with the same depth in the liquid. Measure the spacing and depth with the ruler and keep them the same.

3. **Load line.** Clip the 10 kΩ resistor across the cell, wait 10 s, read $V$. Remove it and let the cell recover for 20 s. Repeat with 4.7 kΩ, 2.2 kΩ, 1 kΩ, 470 Ω, 220 Ω and 100 Ω.
4. **Repeat** steps 2–3 for the other two cells.
5. **Series and parallel (optional).** Join two lemon halves and two vinegar cups in series (copper of one to zinc of the next) and read the open-circuit voltage. Try the LED: long leg towards the copper end. Then join two vinegar cups in parallel and measure their load line with the 470 Ω and 4.7 kΩ resistors.
6. Enter the readings in `my_cells.csv` with columns `cell,R_ohm,V_V`, writing `open` for the open-circuit reading (see `cells_example.csv`).

## Common mistakes

- **Taking the open-circuit voltage as the working voltage.** Under load a lemon cell loses most of its EMF inside itself; the load line shows how much.
- **Measuring current with the meter in series on a lemon.** The ammeter range adds its own resistance and the cell polarises; calculate $I = V/R$ from voltages instead.
- **Not letting the cell recover.** Readings taken right after a heavy load are low; return to open circuit for 20 s between loads.
- **Changing the electrode spacing between runs.** $r$ depends strongly on geometry; compare electrolytes only at the same spacing and depth.
- **Using $\log_{10}$ with 25.7 mV.** $RT/F$ = 25.69 mV goes with $\ln$; with $\log_{10}$ use 59.16 mV, divided by $n$.
- **Trusting the intercept more than the slope.** Junction potentials and activity coefficients move the intercept by millivolts; calibrate with standards.

## Key takeaways

- A real cell is an EMF $E$ in series with an internal resistance $r$: $V = E - Ir$.
- A load line (V against I = V/R) gives $E$ as the intercept and $r$ from the slope, with uncertainties.
- Maximum power, $E^2/4r$, is delivered when the load matches the internal resistance; series cells add both $E$ and $r$.
- Potentiometry measures at near-zero current so the reading is the thermodynamic potential.
- The Nernst slope, 59.16/n mV per decade at 25 °C, is how a voltage becomes a concentration; a low slope signals a failing electrode.
