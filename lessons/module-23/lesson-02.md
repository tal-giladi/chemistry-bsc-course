# 23.2 · Thermochemistry: enthalpy of neutralisation and solution

In lesson 08.7 you built a foam-cup calorimeter, calibrated it and measured how much heat baking soda absorbs when it dissolves. This lesson is the physical chemistry lab version of the same idea. You measure the heat of a classic reaction, the neutralisation of a strong acid by a strong base, in a simulation. At home you measure a reaction that gets cold instead of hot, citric acid with baking soda, and two salts dissolving. For each one you first predict the answer from Hess's law, then measure it, then explain the difference.

## Why it matters

A pharmaceutical plant cools its reactor during a neutralisation step; if the cooling is sized for the wrong ΔH, the batch boils. Instant cold packs work because one salt dissolves endothermically. Effervescent tablets are citric acid and bicarbonate, and they chill the water they fizz in. A prediction from tables plus a quick calorimeter check is how a chemist tells whether a heat effect is safe, useful, or a surprise.

## Core ideas

> [!NOTE]
> The thermochemistry here is stable. Formation enthalpies are from OpenStax Appendix G (NaHCO₃(s), −950.8 kJ/mol, as in lesson 08.7); citric acid ionisation enthalpies from Goldberg et al. (2002); the CO₂ solution enthalpy from the NIST WebBook Henry's-law data; all checked 2026-10-17. Tables for Epsom salt disagree by a few kJ/mol. The example data are generated by `make_data.py`, not measured.

### What a calorimetry lab measures

The method is the one from lesson [08.7](../module-08/lesson-07.md). Calibrate the cup with hot and cold water to find its heat capacity $C_\text{cal}$. Record the temperature before and after mixing, fit straight lines and extrapolate both to the mixing time to get $\Delta T$. Then the heat is

$$q = -\left(m_\text{liquid}\,c + m_\text{solid}\,c_\text{solid} + C_\text{cal}\right)\Delta T$$

and the enthalpy change per mole of the limiting reagent is $\Delta H = q/n$. The cup works at constant pressure, so $q$ is an enthalpy (lesson [13.2](../module-13/lesson-02.md)). The sign convention: a cup that cools means the process absorbed heat, so $\Delta H > 0$.

### Neutralisation: one reaction hiding behind many

Mix hydrochloric acid with sodium hydroxide and the cup warms. Mix nitric acid with potassium hydroxide and it warms by the same amount. That is because strong acids and bases are fully ionised; Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻ and NO₃⁻ are **spectator ions** that take no part. The only reaction is

$$\mathrm{H^+(aq) + OH^-(aq) \rightarrow H_2O(l)} \qquad \Delta H^\circ = -285.83 - (-230.0) = -55.8\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

For a weak acid HA, the acid must also give up its proton, so $\Delta_\text{neut}H = -55.8 + \Delta_\text{ion}H(\text{HA})$. Ionisation enthalpies of carboxylic acids are only a few kJ/mol, so vinegar and citric acid neutralise with almost the same heat. Ammonium ion, with a large ionisation enthalpy, gives much less.

### Dissolving: two big numbers that nearly cancel

Dissolving a salt breaks the crystal (costs energy) and surrounds the ions with water (releases energy). The difference is small and can have either sign. From Appendix G:

| solid | reaction | $\Delta_\text{sol}H^\circ$ (kJ/mol) |
|---|---|---|
| NaCl | NaCl(s) → Na⁺ + Cl⁻ | −240.1 − 167.2 + 411.2 = +3.9 |
| NaHCO₃ | NaHCO₃(s) → Na⁺ + HCO₃⁻ | −240.1 − 691.11 + 950.8 = +19.6 |
| MgSO₄·7H₂O (Epsom salt) | → Mg²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ + 7H₂O | about +12 to +16 |
| MgSO₄ (anhydrous) | → Mg²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ | about −91 |

The last two rows matter at home. The crystal water of Epsom salt is already "hydrating" the ions, so dissolving the heptahydrate takes in heat; the dry salt gives out a lot. Check the label: pharmacy Epsom salt is the heptahydrate.

### Citric acid and baking soda by Hess's law

Citric acid (H₃Cit) has three acidic protons. With baking soda the overall reaction is

$$\mathrm{H_3C_6H_5O_7(aq) + 3\,NaHCO_3(s) \rightarrow Na_3C_6H_5O_7(aq) + 3\,H_2O(l) + 3\,CO_2(g)}$$

At home you use citric acid in excess, so bicarbonate is limiting and the acid gives up mainly its first and second protons. Break the reaction into steps whose enthalpies you know, per mole of NaHCO₃:

1. Dissolve the baking soda: +19.6 kJ/mol.
2. Take a proton from citric acid: +4.07 kJ/mol for the first proton, +2.23 for the second (Goldberg et al.). With the amounts in this lesson, 58 % of the protons come from the first step and 42 % from the second, an average of +3.30.
3. Make water and gas: $\mathrm{H^+ + HCO_3^- \rightarrow H_2O(l) + CO_2(g)}$, $\Delta H = -285.83 - 393.51 + 691.11 = +11.77$ kJ/mol.

Sum: **+34.7 kJ per mole of NaHCO₃**. The reaction is strongly endothermic, yet it runs by itself. It is driven by entropy: every bicarbonate releases a gas molecule, and $T\Delta S$ beats $\Delta H$ (lesson [08.2](../module-08/lesson-02.md)).

One correction. Not all the CO₂ escapes; some stays dissolved, and dissolving CO₂ releases about 19.9 kJ/mol (from the temperature dependence of its Henry's-law constant). Weigh the cup before and after: the mass loss is the CO₂ that left. If a fraction $f$ escaped,

$$\Delta H_\text{obs} = \Delta H_\text{gas} + (1 - f)(-19.9\ \text{kJ/mol})$$

so you can correct the measurement to "all gas" before comparing it with Hess.

### What can go wrong

- **Undissolved citric acid.** Citric acid dissolving also cools the water. Dissolve it first and wait until the temperature is steady, so the run measures only the reaction.
- **Evaporation.** Every CO₂ bubble leaves saturated with water vapour, and evaporating water cools the cup. It makes the reaction look slightly more endothermic.
- **A biased cup constant.** As in 08.7, heat lost while pouring hot water makes $C_\text{cal}$ too large.

## Worked example

### 1. Neutralisation in the simulation

The simulation mixes 50.0 mL of 1.00 M HCl with 50.0 mL of 1.00 M NaOH, both at 21.0 °C, in a cup with $C_\text{cal}$ = 35 J/K. The extrapolated rise is +6.10 K. Treat the 100 g of dilute solution as water.

$$q = -(100.0 \times 4.184 + 35) \times 6.10 = -2766\ \text{J}, \qquad \Delta_\text{neut}H = \frac{-2766\ \text{J}}{0.0500\ \text{mol}} = -55.3\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

Within 1 % of −55.8. The remaining gap is the approximation that the salt solution has the density and heat capacity of water.

### 2. Citric acid and baking soda, from the example data

4.00 g citric acid (0.0208 mol) is dissolved in 100.0 g of water and left to settle. Then 3.00 g NaHCO₃ (0.0357 mol, the limiting reagent: it needs only 0.0119 mol of acid) is added. The extrapolated change is −2.54 K, and the calibration gave $C_\text{cal}$ = 47 J/K.

$$q = (104.0 \times 4.184 + 3.00 \times 1.0 + 47) \times 2.54 = 1233\ \text{J absorbed}, \qquad \Delta H_\text{obs} = \frac{+1233}{0.0357} = +34.6\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

The cup lost 1.45 g: $1.45/44.01 = 0.0329$ mol of CO₂ out of 0.0357, so $f$ = 0.92. Corrected: $\Delta H_\text{gas} = 34.6 + 0.08 \times 19.9 = +36.1 \pm 1.1$ kJ/mol, against the Hess prediction of +34.7. The difference is 1.4 kJ/mol, just over one standard uncertainty. Both systematic errors above push the same way: a cup constant that is too large, and evaporative cooling by the bubbles. A good report names them and does not stop at "human error".

## Hands-on

What you need: the calorimeter from lesson 08.7 (two nested foam cups, cardboard lid with a hole, digital thermometer with 0.1 °C display), a kitchen scale (0.01 g ideally), a kettle, a timer, food-grade citric acid (sold for cooking and descaling; check the label says anhydrous, or use 210.14 g/mol for the monohydrate), baking soda, table salt, Epsom salt from a pharmacy (magnesium sulfate heptahydrate), safety glasses, the [Module 23 lab folder](../../labs/module-23/) and the [lab report template](../../templates/lab-report.md). Time: about 2 hours.

> [!WARNING]
> Wear safety glasses. Citric acid powder irritates the eyes, skin and airways (PubChem lists H315, H319, H335); do not breathe the dust and rinse any splash with plenty of water. The reaction fizzes: keep the cup on a tray and no more than half full. Heat the calibration water only to about 45 °C, hand-hot. **Never let citric acid or any acid near bleach**: together they release chlorine gas. Do this lab on a different day from the bleach experiment of lesson 23.4, or rinse and put away everything first. ### Part B — home calorimetry (60 min)

1. **Calibrate** exactly as in lesson 08.7, Part 2, steps 1–4: 100.0 g of room-temperature water, readings every 30 s, at 3 minutes pour in about 50 g of water at about 45 °C, read for 7 more minutes. Record the masses.
2. **Prepare the acid.** Dissolve 4.00 g of citric acid in 100.0 g of water in the dry calorimeter. Stir until no crystals remain and wait 5 minutes: it cools as it dissolves. Weigh the cup with lid and solution.
3. **Reaction run.** Read every 30 s for 3 minutes. At 3:00 start adding 3.00 g of baking soda in small portions over about a minute, stirring gently. Read every 30 s until 10:30. Weigh the cup, lid and solution again: the loss is CO₂.
4. **Table salt.** Fresh 100.0 g of water; three minutes of readings; at 3:00 tip in 5.00 g of salt and stir until dissolved; read to 10:30.
5. **Epsom salt.** The same with 10.00 g of Epsom salt.
6. Type everything into `my_calorimetry.csv` with columns `run,t_s,T_C` and runs named `calibration`, `reaction`, `nacl`, `epsom` (see `calorimetry_example.csv`).

How you know it worked: steady readings (drift under 0.1 °C per minute) before each addition; the reaction drops by about 2.5 K, table salt by under 1 K, Epsom salt by about 1 K; the cup loses 1.3–1.5 g during the reaction.

<details><summary>Reference solution and what a good discussion says</summary>

`cup_heat_capacity` returns `m_hot * C_WATER * (T_hot - T_final) / (T_final - T_cold) - m_cold * C_WATER`. `enthalpy_per_mol` multiplies `(liquid * C_WATER + m_solid * c_solid + C_cup)` by `(T_before - T_after)`, divides by `m_solid / molar_mass` and by 1000. `all_gas_value` computes `f = (mass_loss / M_CO2) / (m_solid / M_NaHCO3)` and returns `(dH_obs - (1 - f) * DH_CO2_SOLN, f)`. Full code: `lesson02_calorimetry_solution.py`.

A good discussion: the NaCl run has the largest *relative* error, because a 0.8 K change read on a 0.1 °C display is only eight steps; using 10 g would halve it. The reaction value is a little above Hess because the cup constant is biased high (heat lost while pouring) and because bubbles carry away water vapour. The Epsom salt agrees with the table only once you know which table: values from +12 to +16 kJ/mol are quoted. None of this trains handling a real calorimeter or a Dewar flask; it trains the analysis.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the peak temperature.** The lowest reading after a slow reaction is not $T_\text{after}$; extrapolate to the mixing time.
- **Dividing by the wrong moles.** The limiting reagent here is baking soda. Dividing by moles of citric acid gives a number three times larger that answers a different question.
- **Forgetting the dissolved CO₂.** Without the mass-loss correction the result is 1–2 kJ/mol low.
- **Anhydrous versus hydrate.** Anhydrous MgSO₄ heats the water strongly; the heptahydrate cools it. Use the molar mass that matches the label.
- **Sign errors.** A cooling cup means $q$ absorbed by the process is positive and $\Delta H > 0$. Write the sign rule at the top of your notebook page.
- **Adding the baking soda all at once.** It foams over and the mass loss then includes liquid, not only CO₂.

## Key takeaways

- Strong acid plus strong base is always H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O, −55.8 kJ/mol; spectator ions do not matter.
- Enthalpies of solution are small differences between lattice and hydration energies and can be positive or negative; hydrates and anhydrous salts differ hugely.
- Hess's law predicts citric acid + baking soda at +34.7 kJ per mole of NaHCO₃: an endothermic reaction driven by the entropy of the gas it releases.
- Weighing the cup before and after turns the CO₂ that stayed dissolved into a correction instead of an unknown error.
- A calorimetry result is finished only when it has an uncertainty and a named reason for any gap to the prediction.
