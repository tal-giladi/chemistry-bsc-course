# Instructor notes — 19.2 Fluorescence and atomic spectroscopy

- Objective: the learner explains fluorescence sensitivity and its linear limit (A < 0.01), fits a Stern–Volmer constant and predicts quenching, chooses among flame AAS, furnace AAS, ICP-OES and ICP-MS, names the fix for chemical, ionisation and spectral interferences, and uses the Boltzmann factor to explain why emission needs heat.
- Likely confusion: fluorescence intensity depends on source power, absorbance does not; "ionisation interference" is fixed by adding an element that ionises more easily (counter-intuitive); hollow-cathode lamps are element-specific.
- Misconception: "the hotter the flame, the better for every element". Hot flames ionise alkali metals; emission and absorption depend on temperature in different ways.
- Analogy: absorbance is spotting one missing light bulb in a stadium full of lit bulbs; fluorescence is spotting one lit bulb in a dark stadium.
- Expected hands-on result: K_SV ≈ 129 L/mol, intercept 0.99; 82 % at 100 ppm NaCl, 31 % at 1000 ppm; Na N*/N0 1.7e-4 (2500 K), 9.2e-2 (7000 K); Zn 6.2e-12 (2500 K), 2.0e-4 (7000 K); Zn reaches 1e-6 near 4500 K. Method choices: flame AAS + La; furnace or ICP-MS with background correction; ICP-MS; fluorescence with dilution and quench check.
- Extension: point out that sodium in an ICP is mostly ionised, so the Boltzmann ratio for atoms overstates its emission; ask why ICP-OES often uses ion lines. Forward to 25.2 (tonic water) and 25.3 (AAS and emission sims).
