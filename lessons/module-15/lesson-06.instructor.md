# Instructor notes — 15.6 Elimination: E1 and E2

- Objective: the learner writes E2 and E1 mechanisms and rate laws, predicts the major alkene (Zaitsev, Hofmann, anti-periplanar/trans-diaxial), ranks alkene stability from NIST ΔfH°, and chooses among SN1, SN2, E1 and E2.
- Likely confusion: α versus β carbon; why E2 needs anti-periplanar geometry; strong nucleophile versus strong base; kinetic product ratio versus equilibrium ratio.
- Misconception: "Zaitsev's rule is about product stability at equilibrium"; "E2 can happen from any conformation of a cyclohexane".
- Analogy: a strong base is a pickpocket working the edge of a crowd (it takes the outer H), a good nucleophile is someone pushing to the centre (it reaches the carbon); a bulky base is a pickpocket in a big coat who can only reach the outermost pockets.
- Expected hands-on result: anti-periplanar H–C3–C2–Br 180°; methyls anti (E path) or gauche (Z path); trans-1-bromo-2-methylcyclohexane diequatorial has no anti H, diaxial chair has only the C6 H. Script: (E)-2-butene 2 substituents, −10.80 kJ/mol, 76.8 %; (Z) 2, −7.70, 22.0 %; 1-butene 1, −0.63, 1.3 %. Drills: butanenitrile (SN2); propene (E2, bulky); propene (E2); tert-butyl alcohol + some 2-methylpropene (SN1/E1); cyclohexene (E1); neomenthyl → 1-menthene fast.
- Extension: add 2-methyl-1-butene and 2-methyl-2-butene ΔfH° values from the NIST WebBook to `butenes_nist.csv` (check each page exists) and rerun for 2-bromo-2-methylbutane; compare the equilibrium share with the 70 : 30 E2 result.
