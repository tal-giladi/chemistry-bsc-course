# Speed of sound and the heat-capacity ratio of air

> [!CAUTION]
> Keep sounds at a comfortable volume: finger snaps, taps or quiet noise, never a speaker held to your ear or played loudly. Cut tubes with a saw or knife on a board, cutting away from your body. Do not heat air or tubes with flames or heaters; use rooms, a balcony, or a cool morning and a warm afternoon to change the temperature. No chemicals are used.

In 30.3 you measured the speed of sound in a tube with a phone and turned it into the heat-capacity ratio γ of air, a classic physical chemistry experiment. Kinetic theory predicts that the speed of sound in an ideal gas grows with the square root of the absolute temperature and depends a little on humidity. This project tests that prediction over the range of temperatures you can reach at home. It is a safe home-measurement project with no chemicals.

## Question

Over the air temperatures you can reach at home (for example 10–35 °C), does the measured speed of sound follow $c = \sqrt{\gamma R T / M}$ with $\gamma$ = 1.40 and the molar mass of moist air, within your uncertainty, and is your γ the same at every temperature?

## Why it is worth doing

One measurement of γ can agree with 1.40 by luck. A series over temperature tests the model itself: the slope of $c^2$ against $T$ gives γ/M independently of any single point, and the humidity correction becomes visible on warm, humid days. It trains careful acoustics, a calibrated end correction, and error propagation.

## What you need

- The 30.3 kit: a phone with phyphox, a tube closed at one end with a movable plug, a tape measure, a thermometer and a hygrometer (or the humidity from a local weather service, with its time and place noted).
- Places or times at different temperatures: an air-conditioned room, a balcony in the morning and afternoon, a cool stairwell.

## Method

1. Known-answer test: repeat the 30.3 length series in one room and reproduce your earlier $c$ and end correction.
2. Pilot: two length series in one place to get the scatter of $c$; decide the repeats per temperature (Lesson [33.2](../../lessons/module-33/lesson-02.md)).
3. Main run: length series at five or more temperatures, in a shuffled order over several days, recording temperature, humidity and pressure each time. Let the tube reach the air temperature first.
4. Compute $c$ for each series, the molar mass of moist air for each, and fit $c^2$ against $T$; compare γ at each temperature and from the slope.

## Validation

At each temperature the resonances must follow the 1 : 3 : 5 pattern; the end correction should not change with temperature. Your room-temperature γ must agree with 30.3.

## Hours budget

Reading 25 · setup and pilot 20 · measurements 50 (about 45 minutes per length series including setup) · analysis 35 · report 30 · poster 10 · feedback 10.

## What it cannot show

The temperature range is narrow, so curvature is hard to test; air in the tube may not be at the thermometer's temperature; the humidity value may come from a distant station. Wall losses in narrow tubes slightly lower the speed. A real lab would use a thermostatted tube and a signal generator.
